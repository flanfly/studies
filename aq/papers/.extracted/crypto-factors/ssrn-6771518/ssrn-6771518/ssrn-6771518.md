### **Abstract**

This paper investigates how characteristic-based factors that account for higherorder moments developed in the equity asset class can be applied to construct optimized portfolios in cryptocurrency markets. Using a fourth-order Taylor expansion of expected utility, the analysis extends traditional mean–variance methods to capture the strongly non-Gaussian features of digital asset returns. Empirical results show that characteristics such as size, momentum, and tokenomics systematically load higher-order risks and help explain cross-sectional variation in crypto returns. Incorporating these moment exposures into the optimization process yields substantial improvements in risk-adjusted performance and portfolio stability, particularly in environments marked by asymmetry, fat tails, and liquidity frictions. Overall, the findings demonstrate that higher-moment portfolio construction transfers robustly from equities to crypto assets and provides a tractable framework for modeling their unique distributional properties.

### **1 Introduction**

Over the past decade, cryptocurrencies have evolved from speculative curiosities into a significant and volatile asset class with global market capitalization exceeding \$1 trillion. Yet, despite their increasing institutional adoption and economic relevance, the foundational question of how to model risk and return in cryptocurrency markets remains unresolved. Traditional asset pricing tools, such as mean-variance optimization and linear factor models, struggle to capture the extreme non-normality, structural idiosyncrasies, and behavioral underpinnings that characterize digital assets.

A growing literature seeks to understand the cross-section of cryptocurrency returns through the lens of empirical factor models. Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu [\(2022\)](#page-89-0) identify market, size, and momentum factors that exhibit significant explanatory power for crypto returns, analogous to the Fama-French model in equities. Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu [\(2022\)](#page-89-0) construct a broader asset pricing framework for digital assets by incorporating blockchain-based variables such as network activity, supply growth, and investor attention. These studies demonstrate that systematic return predictability exists in crypto markets and that factor-based structures can partially explain risk premia in a space devoid of traditional valuation anchors.

Although these empirical approaches represent an important step forward, they remain grounded in second-moment frameworks and often assume normality or linearity in factor exposures. Such assumptions are particularly tenuous in cryptocurrency markets, where return distributions are persistently skewed, fat-tailed, and subject to sudden regime shifts. In addition, crypto assets lack cash flows, institutional governance, and regulatory clarity, leading to unique dynamics such as dilution through token issuance, speculative feedback loops, and protocol-level risks. These features suggest that investor preferences in crypto may differ materially from those assumed in classical models, placing greater weight on downside protection, tail asymmetry, and higher-moment risks.

This paper introduces a new portfolio construction framework that addresses these challenges by incorporating investor preferences for higherorder moments mean, variance, skewness, and kurtosis into characteristicbased factor models. Building on the Constant Absolute Risk Aversion (CARA) utility function, we derive a closed-form expected utility expression via a fourth-order Taylor expansion. This allows us to embed co-skewness and co-kurtosis directly into the portfolio optimization problem, capturing aversion to asymmetry and fat tails in a theoretically grounded and tractable manner. To address the well-known instability of higher-order moment estimation in high-dimensional settings, we develop a shrinkage-based regularization technique that blends sample-based moment tensors with structured priors. This approach ensures robustness and feasibility when optimizing portfolios across a large cross-section of crypto assets with limited historical data.

Empirically, we implement our framework in the cryptocurrency universe, constructing long-short portfolios based on a set of firm-level characteristics including size, momentum, liquidity, and a novel *tokenomics* factor. The tokenomics characteristic captures the supply-side dynamics of crypto assets, such as inflation schedules and dilution risk-dimensions uniquely relevant in decentralized digital economies. Our results show that portfolios optimized under this higher-moment utility framework consistently outperform traditional mean-variance benchmarks in terms of Sharpe ratio, drawdown protection, and out-of-sample performance.

Importantly, the gains from incorporating higher-order moments are most pronounced during periods of market stress, when tail risk dominates investor concern and when conventional models often fail. These results suggest that a utility-consistent, tail-aware portfolio optimization approach is essential for accurately modeling risk-return tradeoffs in crypto markets.

In contrast to existing factor pricing models that emphasize linear exposure to empirically derived factors, our framework provides a decisiontheoretic foundation that aligns portfolio construction with investor preferences for asymmetry and tail risk. By directly incorporating co-skewness and co-kurtosis into the optimization objective and stabilizing estimation through shrinkage, we offer a robust and scalable approach that can extend beyond crypto to other markets exhibiting non-normal return distributions.

### <span id="page-5-0"></span>**2 Literature review**

Research on portfolio optimization has long emphasized the trade-off between return and risk, formalized through mean-variance frameworks pioneered by Markowitz. However, these models assume normally distributed returns and overlook investors' preferences for asymmetry and fat tails. To address these limitations, a substantial body of work has extended utilitybased frameworks to incorporate higher-order moments.

The foundational contribution by Jondeau and Rockinger [\(2004\)](#page-89-1) extends utility maximization by incorporating co-skewness and co-kurtosis into a Taylor-expanded CARA utility function, enabling optimization beyond the second moment. Harvey and Siddique [\(2000\)](#page-89-2) show that skewness and kurtosis are not only priced in equity markets but also significantly affect investor behavior and utility. In high-dimensional settings, DeMiguel, Garlappi, Nogales and Uppal [\(2009\)](#page-88-0) advocate the use of shrinkage estimators to stabilize higher-order moment estimation. These studies collectively establish that investor utility is better aligned with higher moment strategies than with mean-variance-only approaches.

In equity markets, Kozak, Nagel and Santosh [\(2020\)](#page-89-3) provide a robust framework for extracting characteristic-based factors through latent risk pricing models, though they do not incorporate higher moments. Green, Hand and Zhang [\(2017\)](#page-89-4) explore the pricing power of firm characteristics but rely on linear regression-based factor sorting. These studies underscore the importance of characteristics in explaining cross-sectional return variation, though they remain confined to the first two moments of return distributions.

Momentum, in particular, emerges as a dominant driver, reflecting behavioral biases and speculative dynamics inherent to crypto trading. Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu [\(2022\)](#page-89-0) further validate the explanatory power of these factors in a large cross-sectional setting, though their framework remains linear and regression-based, assuming expected return maximization rather than utility optimization. Makarov and Schoar [\(2020\)](#page-90-1) investigate arbitrage inefficiencies in crypto markets, showing that fragmented exchanges and the absence of centralized clearing lead to persistent price discrepancies. These inefficiencies are most acute during liquidity shocks, although institutional participation has reduced some of these frictions. Borri and Shakhnov [\(2022\)](#page-88-6) extend this analysis by showing that cross-exchange Bitcoin price differentials reflect risk premiums that spike in low-liquidity environments, linking market frictions directly to risk-return tradeoffs.

Crypto markets exhibit extreme skewness and kurtosis, making higherorder moments critical for accurate risk modeling. Borri [\(2019\)](#page-88-7) show that return distributions are fat-tailed and prone to extreme negative shocks, which standard mean-variance models fail to capture. To mitigate this, they advocate risk measures that explicitly account for higher-order moments, aligning more closely with real-world investor concerns. Despite this, the broader literature on crypto asset pricing has largely ignored moment-based utility optimization and the role of tail risks in portfolio construction.

While existing research identifies important drivers of cryptocurrency returns—such as momentum, attention, size, and tokenomics—most frameworks are linear, regression-based, and grounded in expected return maximization. They overlook investor preferences for skewness and kurtosis, which are critical in non-Gaussian markets like crypto. Furthermore, despite evidence that higher-order moments materially affect performance and tail risk, there has been little effort to incorporate these moments into utilitybased optimization or to examine their impact on the Stochastic Discount Factor (SDF) spanning.

This study addresses these gaps by incorporating higher-order moments into portfolio construction via a Taylor-expanded CARA utility function within a characteristic-based factor framework. We extend the Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu [\(2022\)](#page-89-0) three-factor model—market, size, and momentum into a utility-maximizing framework that reflects skewness and kurtosis preferences. We also incorporate crypto-specific characteristics such as tokenomics to test their incremental contribution. In doing so, we bridge the gap between cross-sectional factor models and higher moment utility optimization in the context of structurally fragmented and fat-tailed cryptocurrency markets.

# <span id="page-8-0"></span>**3 Methodology: Characteristic-Based Portfolio Construction in Cryptocurrency Markets**

We employ a characteristic-based portfolio construction framework that incorporates higher-order moments and shrinkage regularization to construct cryptocurrency portfolio strategies. This approach provides a consistent basis for comparing performance and risk characteristics across asset classes.

The framework is designed to construct long–short portfolios that exploit cross-sectional asset characteristics, incorporate investor preferences for higher-order moments, and enhance out-of-sample robustness through shrinkage regularization. We begin with a mean–variance benchmark as the foundational model and then sequentially introduce two extensions: (i) a shrinkage-based regularization scheme to control estimation risk in highdimensional settings, and (ii) a utility-based optimization that integrates higher-order moments—specifically skewness and kurtosis—via a Taylorseries expansion of the CARA utility function.

This sequential augmentation enables a unified analysis of how characteristicbased signals translate into return, risk, and welfare performance when accounting for non-Gaussian return distributions and estimation uncertainty in cryptocurrency markets.

### 3.3 CARA Utility with Higher-Order Moments

To move beyond mean-variance preferences, we adopt a utility-theoretic approach grounded in Constant Absolute Risk Aversion (CARA) preferences, where investor utility is given by:

$$U(W) = -\exp(-\lambda W).$$

Where,  $\lambda$  measures the investor's constant absolute risk aversion and  $W$  is end-of-period wealth.

Since the expected utility  $\mathbb{E}[U(W)]$  is generally not available in closed form, we approximate it using a fourth-order Taylor expansion around the expected terminal wealth  $\bar{W}$ , where  $\bar{W} = \mathbb{E}[W]$ :

$$\mathbb{E}[U(W)] \approx -\exp(-\lambda \bar{W}) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \sigma_p^2 - \frac{\lambda^3}{6} s_p^3 + \frac{\lambda^4}{24} \kappa_p^4 \right],$$

where  $\sigma_p^2$ ,  $s_p^3$ , and  $\kappa_p^4$  denote the variance, skewness, and kurtosis of the end-of-period return, respectively.

### 3.4 Utility-Based Optimization with Moment Preferences

We next solve the investor's problem of selecting portfolio weights that maximize expected utility under higher order moment considerations:

$$\begin{aligned} \max_{\alpha} \quad & -\exp(-\lambda \bar{W}) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \sigma_p^2 - \frac{\lambda^3}{6} s_p^3 + \frac{\lambda^4}{24} \kappa_p^4 \right] \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \alpha^\top \mathbf{1} = 0, \quad \alpha^\top c = 1 \end{aligned}$$

Let  $\mu_p = \alpha^\top \mu$  denote the portfolio mean return. The higher-order mo-

ments are given by

$$\sigma_p^2 = \alpha^\top M_2 \alpha,$$

$$s_p^3 = \alpha^\top M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha),$$

$$\kappa_p^4 = \alpha^\top M_4 (\alpha \otimes \alpha \otimes \alpha),$$

where

 $M_2$  : covariance matrix,

 $M_3$  : co-skewness tensor, *and*

 $M_4$  : co-kurtosis tensor.

Details are provided in Appendix A.

Introducing Lagrange multipliers  $\kappa$  and  $\theta$  for the linear constraints, the associated Lagrangian becomes:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\alpha, \kappa, \theta) = & -\exp(-\lambda \mu_p) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \alpha^\top M_2 \alpha - \frac{\lambda^3}{6} \alpha^\top M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha) + \frac{\lambda^4}{24} \alpha^\top M_4 (\alpha \otimes \alpha \otimes \alpha) \right] \\ & - \kappa(\alpha^\top c - 1) - \theta(\alpha^\top \mathbf{1}) \end{aligned}$$

### 3.6 Fama–MacBeth Estimation with Characteristic Factors

To benchmark the performance of the higher moments portfolio model, we implement a characteristic-based Fama and MacBeth procedure following the methodology of Lioui and Misra (2023). In contrast to the classical two-pass approach of Fama and MacBeth (1973), which relies on first stage time series regressions to estimate factor loadings, the Lioui framework treats cross-sectional characteristics as primitive pricing variables. Accordingly, factor premia are recovered directly from single-stage cross-sectional regressions of returns on lagged characteristics, thereby avoiding the estimation noise associated with betas and factor proxies.

#### Cross-Sectional Estimation

For each month  $t$ , we estimate the regression

$$R_{i,t} = \alpha_t + \lambda_{C,t} C_{i,t-1} + \varepsilon_{i,t}, \quad i \in \mathcal{I}_t, \quad (2)$$

where  $R_{i,t}$  denotes the excess return on asset  $i$  in month  $t$ ,  $C_{i,t-1}$  is the lagged characteristic under consideration (e.g., size, value, momentum, liquidity, volatility, growth, or any other cross-sectional signal), and  $\mathcal{I}_t$  is the set of assets with complete data at time  $t$ . The slope coefficient  $\lambda_{C,t}$  captures the realized characteristic premium in month  $t$ , producing a time series of monthly estimates,

$$\{\lambda_{C,t}\}_{t=1}^T.$$

The unconditional price of risk associated with characteristic  $C$  is obtained by time-series averaging:

$$\bar{\lambda}_C = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \lambda_{C,t}. \quad (3)$$

Standard errors are computed from the time-series dispersion of the monthly estimates,

$$\widehat{\text{SE}}(\bar{\lambda}_C) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T(T-1)} \sum_{t=1}^T (\lambda_{C,t} - \bar{\lambda}_C)^2}. \quad (4)$$

A statistically significant  $\bar{\lambda}_C$  indicates that the characteristic commands a nonzero price of risk and helps explain the cross-section of expected returns.

### 3.7 Construction of Asset Characteristics

The conditioning characteristics  $\mathbf{c}$  include variables motivated by both traditional and crypto-specific cross-sectional return predictors:

- • **Size:** Logarithm of end-of-month market capitalization.
- • **1- month Momentum:** Return over the most recent month ( $t-1$  to  $t$ ), measured as the cumulative percentage change in each cryptocurrency's price. This short-horizon momentum factor captures near-term continuation patterns commonly observed in digital asset markets, where price dynamics are often driven by liquidity shocks, investor attention, and speculative trading activity. The 1-month specification is particularly relevant in the crypto context, as return autocorrelation tends to decay rapidly relative to traditional asset classes, making short-term momentum a more informative predictor of subsequent performance.
- • **Tokenomics:** Twelve-month percent change in circulating supply, computed as:

$$\text{Tokenomics}_{i,t} = \frac{S_{i,t} - S_{i,t-12}}{S_{i,t-12}},$$

where  $S_{i,t}$  is the circulating supply of asset  $i$  at time  $t$ .

While size and momentum extend predictors commonly used in equities, tokenomics reflects protocol-level monetary policy, including inflation from emissions or deflation through burns and vesting constraints.

### **3.8 Market Frictions and Bias Corrections**

Cryptocurrency markets present structural limitations that affect portfolio construction:

- **Short-sale constraints:** Shorting is generally infeasible for smaller tokens. Where applicable, portfolios are either constrained to longonly allocations or hedged using large-cap proxies such as Bitcoin or Ethereum.
- **Delisting and survivorship bias:** Assets are retained in the dataset through their final available trading month. We adjust return aggregations to reflect exits due to project failure, illiquidity, or exchange delisting.
- **Estimation error:** Shrinkage is systematically applied to all moment estimators to improve out-of-sample stability. Parameters are tuned via cross-validation on rolling windows.

## **Cross-Sectional Return Predictors**

The equity market and the crypto market are fundamentally different in several ways, yet they share some similarities in terms of trading dynamics. Here is a comparative analysis.

Historically, equity and crypto markets were uncorrelated. However, since 2020, Bitcoin and major crypto assets have shown a higher correlation with risk assets (like tech stocks in the NASDAQ), mainly due to institutional adoption. During market stress, crypto behaves more like a risk-on asset, experiencing larger drawdowns than equities. Monetary policy impacts both, but equities tend to react more to interest rates and earnings, while crypto reacts to liquidity conditions and broader macro trends. We can construct a long-short portfolio with the equities but most of the crypto coins other than the leading ones like Bitcoin, Erethreum has a liquidity problem and there can be restrictions in constructing long-short strategy with any crypto. We begin with the common factors from Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu [\(2022\)](#page-89-0) . In this paper, three factors - the cryptocurrency size, momentum and tokenomics - capture the expected cross-sectional returns of the cryptocurrency.

We begin by incorporating two foundational characteristics—Size and Momentum, which have been shown to exhibit robust cross-sectional return predictability in both equity and cryptocurrency markets. Empirical studies in digital assets Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu [\(2022\)](#page-89-0) find that analogs of traditional risk factors retain explanatory power, with market capitalization (Size) capturing liquidity and investor attention effects, and Momentum reflecting behavioral biases and delayed information diffusion. These findings motivate the construction of cryptocurrency-specific factor analogs that parallel well-established equity characteristics, while accounting for the unique microstructure and trading dynamics of digital asset markets. Our framework builds on this literature but shifts from a purely regression-based factor pricing model to a utility-maximizing approach that directly constructs characteristic-mimicking portfolios. By embedding Size and Momentum as conditioning variables in a portfolio optimization that incorporates skewness, kurtosis, and regularization, we provide a more investor-centric assessment of the welfare implications of these characteristics. This enables us to go beyond linear factor premia and assess the economic value of characteristics through their contribution to tail risk-adjusted portfolio performance. Our methodology thus offers a structural extension to the risk factor literature in crypto assets, linking observable characteristics to optimal allocations in a manner that is both theoretically grounded and empirically implementable.

### **5.1 Size as Characteristics**

We use a 60-month rolling window of risk-adjusted excess returns to construct optimized portfolio weights, updating each month based on the market capitalization observed in the 60th month. In this setting, Size serves as a conditioning characteristic that reflects liquidity, investor visibility, and the scale of network adoption, key dimensions in cryptocurrency markets where traditional accounting fundamentals are absent. Unlike equities, where size may proxy for balance sheet strength or firm maturity, market capitalization in digital assets captures a combination of speculative interest, exchange listing status, and perceived protocol legitimacy. Prior research Liu, Tsyvinski and Wu [\(2022\)](#page-89-0) documents a persistent size effect in crypto markets, with smaller-cap tokens generating higher average returns, consistent with underreaction, attention-based pricing, and limited arbitrage.

Within our framework, Size enters directly into the optimization constraints, enabling the construction of factor-mimicking portfolios under highermoment utility preferences. This approach moves beyond conventional decile sorting or linear regression loadings by directly testing whether size-conditioned allocations enhance expected utility in environments characterized by non-Gaussian returns, estimation risk, and short-sale constraints.

Empirical comparisons between the CARA and shrinkage-regularized CARA optimizations reveal the contribution of Size to portfolio structure and performance. The shrinkage enhanced optimization exhibits lower kurtosis, indicating reduced exposure to extreme returns and improved tail risk management, critical in the volatile crypto setting. Simultaneously, increased skewness suggests a portfolio tilt toward positively asymmetric return distributions, consistent with selective exposure to smaller-cap tokens with high upside potential. Notably, the improvement in Sharpe ratio confirms that incorporating Size under higher-moment preferences yields superior risk-adjusted returns.

The analysis of portfolio weights across strategies reveals notable differences in concentration and asset selection patterns. Strategies that penalize higher moments, such as CARA utility with shrinkage minus kurtosis or skewness exhibit significantly higher portfolio concentration. These concentrated allocations tend to overweight a small number of tokens. In contrast, traditional CARA strategies with or without shrinkage suggest highly diversified allocations. Despite the broader dispersion, these portfolios consistently allocate to a core group of liquid mid-cap tokens, reflecting their attractive risk-return tradeoffs under higher-moment utility preferences. The presence of consistent top performers across strategies further underscores the robustness of these assets in capturing investor preferred characteristics, particularly when conditioning on size and accounting for estimation stability in high dimensional settings.

Taken together, these findings underscore the role of Size as a meaningful pricing characteristic in crypto asset allocation. Portfolios that condition on Size, particularly when combined with shrinkage and higher-moment objectives, appear more effective in managing downside risk, capturing asymmetric gains, and achieving greater portfolio stability across rolling investment horizons.

To compare the results and benchmark the performance, we perform three tests. The Jobson-Korkie test is used to statistically compare the Sharpe ratios of two investment strategies. Since the Sharpe ratio combines return and risk into a single performance metric, the test evaluates whether the observed difference between two Sharpe ratios is due to chance or reflects a real difference in risk-adjusted performance. The test accounts for estimation error and sampling variability, and a corrected version (Memmel correction) adjusts for the fact that the two strategies may be correlated or based on overlapping time periods. A significant result from this test indicates that one strategy is statistically superior in terms of Sharpe ratio.

The Diebold-Mariano (DM) test, on the other hand, is designed to compare the forecast accuracy of two models. It does this by examining the difference in loss functions—typically squared forecast errors—between the two models over time. If one model consistently makes better predictions (i.e., lower error), the DM test can detect this and determine whether the difference is statistically significant. It is widely used in time-series forecasting and financial modeling to assess whether one approach produces more reliable or efficient predictions than another. Together, these two tests provide complementary views: the Jobson-Korkie test focuses on realized performance (risk-adjusted return), while the Diebold-Mariano test focuses on predictive performance.

### **5.2 1-Month momentum as Characteristics**

The 1-Month momentum factor exhibits distinct behaviors in equity and cryptocurrency markets due to differences in market efficiency, liquidity, and investor composition. In traditional equity markets, short-term momentum effects, such as those observed over a 1-Month period, are generally weak or even exhibit reversal tendencies. Research by Jegadeesh and Titman [\(1993\)](#page-89-8) shows that while momentum strategies perform well over 3 to 12 months, very short-term momentum (such as 1- to 4-week returns) often results in price corrections rather than continued trends. This reversal effect in equities is driven by liquidity and market frictions, where institutional investors adjust positions gradually, leading to temporary price pressures that normalize over time. Additionally, profit-taking behavior among traders further contributes to these short-term reversals, as investors lock in gains after a rapid price increase. Another contributing factor is the bid-ask bounce effect, where stock prices fluctuate due to short-term liquidity imbalances rather than fundamental value changes. As a result, a 1-Month momentum strategy in equities does not typically generate excess returns and may even show negative performance.

In contrast, cryptocurrencies exhibit strong short-term momentum, including over 1- to 4-week periods. A study by Liu, Tsyvinski, and Wu (2022) finds that a 4-week momentum strategy in the cryptocurrency market produces statistically significant positive returns, with excess weekly returns of approximately 2.2 percent long-short strategies. The momentum effect in crypto is more pronounced compared to equities and persists over much shorter time horizons. Several factors contribute to this difference. First, the cryptocurrency market is heavily retail-driven, meaning that a higher proportion of traders engage in momentum-based strategies, chasing past price performance. Additionally, speculative behavior plays a dominant role in crypto trading, as cryptocurrencies generally lack traditional financial fundamentals, making traders more reliant on market trends and technical indicators. Furthermore, the high volatility inherent in the crypto market reinforces momentum effects, as rapid price increases attract more investors, creating self-reinforcing cycles. Unlike equities, where institutional arbitrageurs often exploit momentum inefficiencies, cryptocurrencies operate in a less efficient environment, allowing short-term trends to persist longer.

Overall, the 1-Month momentum factor highlights a fundamental difference between the two asset classes. In equities, short-term price movements are frequently corrected by institutional activity, liquidity constraints, and profit-taking, leading to weak or negative momentum effects over 4-week periods. Conversely, in the cryptocurrency market, the same short-term momentum generates statistically significant positive returns, fueled by speculative trading, high volatility, and a less efficient market structure. This divergence underscores how cryptocurrency markets are more susceptible to short-term price trends, whereas equities exhibit greater mean reversion and efficiency corrections over similar time frames.

### **5.3 Factors Specific to Cryptocurrency Markets**

Cryptocurrency markets exhibit unique risk factors that differentiate them from traditional asset classes such as equities, bonds, and commodities. Unlike equities, which derive their value from financial fundamentals like earnings, dividends, and book value, cryptocurrencies are largely driven by network adoption, investor sentiment, liquidity conditions, and regulatory risks. One of the most important factors in cryptocurrency pricing is network activity and adoption, where metrics such as the number of active addresses, transaction volume, and hash rate (for proof-of-work coins like Bitcoin) serve as indicators of a cryptocurrency's real-world usage and utility. Higher network activity often correlates with higher valuations, as broader adoption increases a cryptocurrency's demand and perceived legitimacy.

Liquidity and trading frictions also play a crucial role in cryptocurrency asset pricing. Unlike equity markets, where liquidity is relatively stable, crypto markets experience frequent liquidity constraints due to fragmented trading across multiple exchanges, large bid-ask spreads, and reliance on stablecoins for trading. Many cryptocurrencies face price fragmentation across different exchanges, creating arbitrage opportunities that can distort pricing. Moreover, exchange-specific liquidity risks arise from differences in trading volumes, with certain exchanges inflating their reported volume, making it difficult to assess true liquidity. The presence of stablecoins, such as USDT and USDC, also affects market stability, as their liquidity influences the overall depth of crypto trading pairs.

Meme-driven speculation, as seen with *Dogecoin* and *Shiba Inu*, underscores the impact of retail investor sentiment. Cryptocurrency pricing is also shaped by tokenomics and supply dynamics, which differ significantly from traditional stock markets. Unlike stocks, where supply remains relatively stable except for buybacks or new issuances, many cryptocurrencies have mechanisms like fixed supply caps, staking rewards, and token burns that directly affect their valuation. Another defining feature of crypto markets is their extreme volatility and market cycles. Furthermore, the loss of private keys or theft due to hacking can lead to permanent asset loss, further increasing the risks associated with crypto custody.

# **Circulating supply and Inflation rate to form Tokenomicsbased factor**

Using Tokenomics as a factor in cryptocurrency portfolio construction is motivated by the unique economic and structural features of digital assets that distinguish them from traditional equities. Unlike stocks, which are backed by firms with cash flows and earnings, crypto tokens derive much of their value from protocol design, supply mechanisms, and incentive structures—collectively referred to as tokenomics. These attributes directly impact investor expectations about scarcity, inflation, governance, and utility, which in turn influence prices and return dynamics.

Traditional factors such as Size or Momentum are inherently marketbased and backward-looking: Size proxies for market capitalization and Momentum reflects recent price trends. In contrast, Tokenomics is a forwardlooking and structural characteristic. It includes information such as maximum supply caps (e.g., Bitcoin), inflation rates (e.g., Ethereum's shift to deflationary supply post-merge), staking rewards, burn mechanisms, token distribution schedules, and governance rights. These elements shape the long-term incentive compatibility of participants in the ecosystem and are exogenous to short-term market fluctuations.

By incorporating Tokenomics into portfolio optimization, we aim to capture a cross-sectional risk premium that reflects these design-level fundamentals. Empirically, as shown in our results, portfolios constrained by Tokenomics exposure tend to show greater persistence in weight allocation, lower turnover, and more stable Sharpe ratios. This suggests that Tokenomics is not only a theoretically grounded factor but also a practically effective dimension for explaining and forecasting crypto returns. Moreover, it opens a pathway for factor-based investing tailored to the digital asset space, where traditional asset pricing models fall short due to the lack of fundamental cash flow anchors.

# Appendix A: Portfolio Construction and Optimization Framework

This appendix outlines the theoretical foundation and computational implementation of a higher-moment-aware portfolio optimization framework. The approach is designed to incorporate skewness and kurtosis, in addition to mean and variance, using a Taylor expansion of the CARA utility function and apply shrinkage regularization to the higher order moments.

In this section, we show the methodology of the long-short portfolio construction with higher order moments. We begin with the classical minimum variance long-short formulation, and extend it to include shrinkage regularization and higher order moment utility optimization under Constant Absolute Risk Aversion (CARA) preferences.

Let  $\mathbf{R}$  denote the vector of asset returns, with mean vector  $\mu = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}]$  and covariance matrix  $\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{R} - \mu)(\mathbf{R} - \mu)^\top]$ . Let  $\alpha$  denote the portfolio weights, and let  $\mathbf{c}$  be a vector of cross-sectional characteristics (e.g., size, momentum). The goal is to construct a long-short portfolio that has the following assumptions.

- • Zero net exposure (Long Short Portfolio):  $\alpha^\top \mathbf{1} = 0$
- • Sum of characteristic weights is 1:  $\alpha^\top \mathbf{c} = 1$

We solve the following constrained optimization problem.

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{\alpha} \quad & \alpha^\top \Sigma \alpha \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \alpha^\top \mathbf{1} = 0 \\ & \alpha^\top \mathbf{c} = 1 \end{aligned}$$

where,  $\mathbf{1}$  is a vector of ones. We form the Lagrangian by introducing Lagrange multipliers  $\lambda_1$  and  $\lambda_2$  for the two constraints and define the Lagrangian function as:

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha, \lambda_1, \lambda_2) = \alpha^\top \Sigma \alpha - \lambda_1(\alpha^\top \mathbf{1}) - \lambda_2(\alpha^\top \mathbf{c} - 1)$$

First-order condition:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \alpha} = 2\Sigma\alpha - \lambda_1\mathbf{1} - \lambda_2c = 0 \Rightarrow \alpha = \frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}(\lambda_1\mathbf{1} + \lambda_2c)$$

Substituting into constraints:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2}(\lambda_1\mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} + \lambda_2\mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}c) &= 0 \\ \frac{1}{2}(\lambda_1c^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} + \lambda_2c^\top\Sigma^{-1}c) &= 1 \end{aligned}$$

Define:

$$A = \mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}, \quad B = \mathbf{1}^\top\Sigma^{-1}c, \quad F = c^\top\Sigma^{-1}c$$

Then the system becomes:

$$\lambda_1A + \lambda_2B = 0$$

$$\lambda_1B + \lambda_2F = 2$$

Solve for  $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ , and plug into:

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}(\lambda_1\mathbf{1} + \lambda_2c)$$

Solving the first equation for  $\lambda_1$ :

$$\lambda_1 = -\frac{B}{A}\lambda_2$$

Substitute into the second equation:

$$\left(-\frac{B}{A}\lambda_2\right)B + \lambda_2F = 2 \Rightarrow -\frac{B^2}{A}\lambda_2 + F\lambda_2 = 2 \Rightarrow \lambda_2\left(F - \frac{B^2}{A}\right) = 2$$

Solving for  $\lambda_2$ :

$$\lambda_2 = \frac{2}{F - \frac{B^2}{A}} = \frac{2A}{AF - B^2}$$

Back-substitute to find  $\lambda_1$ :

$$\lambda_1 = -\frac{B}{A}\lambda_2 = -\frac{2B}{AF - B^2}$$

Final expression for the optimal weights:

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}(\lambda_1\mathbf{1} + \lambda_2c) = \Sigma^{-1}\left(-\frac{B}{AF - B^2}\mathbf{1} + \frac{A}{AF - B^2}c\right)$$

## Shrinkage-Regularized Minimum-Variance Long-Short Portfolio with Characteristic Exposure

Let  $R$  denote the vector of asset returns with mean  $\mu = \mathbb{E}[R]$  and covariance matrix  $\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[(R - \mu)(R - \mu)^\top]$ . Let  $\alpha$  denote the portfolio weights, and let  $c$  be a vector of cross-sectional characteristics (e.g., size, momentum). The objective is to construct a long-short portfolio that is (i) dollar-neutral and (ii) has unit exposure to the characteristic  $c$ , while incorporating shrinkage regularization.

The classical minimum-variance portfolio relies on  $\Sigma^{-1}$ , which can be unstable in high-dimensional settings or when  $\Sigma$  is noisy or ill-conditioned. To mitigate this, we impose an  $\ell_2$  (ridge) penalty on the portfolio weights. The regularization parameter  $\rho \geq 0$  is chosen by the researcher and controls the degree of shrinkage. In contrast, the quantity  $\alpha^\top \alpha = \|\alpha\|^2$  is the magnitude of the portfolio weights being penalized. These two quantities play distinct roles and must not be equated.

Shrinkage penalizes large positions and improves numerical stability by replacing the precision matrix  $\Sigma^{-1}$  with the regularized inverse  $(\Sigma + \rho I)^{-1}$ . Empirical research shows that such regularization reduces turnover, improves out-of-sample performance, and mitigates sensitivity to small eigenvalues of  $\Sigma$ .

## A.1 Shrinkage-Regularized Optimization Problem

The regularized minimum-variance problem is

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{\alpha} \quad & \alpha^{\top} \Sigma \alpha + \rho \|\alpha\|^2 \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \alpha^{\top} \mathbf{1} = 0, \\ & \alpha^{\top} c = 1, \end{aligned}$$

where  $\mathbf{1}$  denotes the vector of ones. Introducing Lagrange multipliers  $\lambda_1$  and  $\lambda_2$  for the two constraints, the Lagrangian becomes

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha, \lambda_1, \lambda_2) = \alpha^{\top} (\Sigma + \rho I) \alpha - \lambda_1 (\alpha^{\top} \mathbf{1}) - \lambda_2 (\alpha^{\top} c - 1).$$

**First-Order Condition.** Differentiating yields

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \alpha} = 2(\Sigma + \rho I) \alpha - \lambda_1 \mathbf{1} - \lambda_2 c = 0,$$

so that

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{2} (\Sigma + \rho I)^{-1} (\lambda_1 \mathbf{1} + \lambda_2 c).$$

Define the regularized precision matrix

$$M = (\Sigma + \rho I)^{-1},$$

and the scalar quantities

$$A = \mathbf{1}^{\top} M \mathbf{1}, \quad B = \mathbf{1}^{\top} M c, \quad F = c^{\top} M c.$$

Substituting the expression for  $\alpha$  into the constraints gives

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_1 A + \lambda_2 B) &= 0, \\ \frac{1}{2}(\lambda_1 B + \lambda_2 F) &= 1.\end{aligned}$$

Solving the linear system yields

$$\lambda_2 = \frac{2A}{AF - B^2}, \quad \lambda_1 = -\frac{2B}{AF - B^2}.$$

Substituting back into the expression for  $\alpha$  gives the closed-form solution:

$$\alpha = M \left( -\frac{B}{AF - B^2} \mathbf{1} + \frac{A}{AF - B^2} c \right).$$

This is the shrinkage regularized minimum variance long short portfolio with zero net investment and unit exposure to the characteristic  $c$ .

## Higher-Order Utility Approximation

The mean–variance formulation is no longer sufficient to span the stochastic discount factor (SDF) once higher-order moments are incorporated. To account for investor preferences over variance, skewness, and kurtosis, we adopt a Constant Absolute Risk Aversion (CARA) utility specification:

$$U(W) = -\exp(-\lambda W), \tag{5}$$

where  $\lambda > 0$  denotes the coefficient of absolute risk aversion and  $W$  is end-of-period wealth. The investor chooses portfolio weights to maximize expected utility  $\mathbb{E}[U(W)]$ . This specification is widely used in the literature due to its tractability and the clear interpretation of the risk-aversion parameter.

Because the expected utility  $\mathbb{E}[U(W)]$  does not admit a closed form, we approximate

it using a fourth-order Taylor expansion around the expected terminal wealth  $\bar{W}$ :

$$\mathbb{E}[U(W)] \approx -\exp(-\lambda\bar{W}) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sigma_p^2 - \frac{\lambda^3}{6}s_p^3 + \frac{\lambda^4}{24}\kappa_p^4 \right], \quad (6)$$

where  $\sigma_p^2$ ,  $s_p^3$ , and  $\kappa_p^4$  denote the variance, skewness, and excess kurtosis of the portfolio return distribution, respectively.

Let  $z_{t+1}$  denote the vector of asset excess returns. The first four moments of the portfolio return are

$$\mu_p = \alpha^\top \mu, \quad (7)$$

$$\sigma_p^2 = \alpha^\top M_2 \alpha, \quad (8)$$

$$s_p^3 = \alpha^\top M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha), \quad (9)$$

$$\kappa_p^4 = \alpha^\top M_4 (\alpha \otimes \alpha \otimes \alpha), \quad (10)$$

where

- •  $M_2$  is the covariance matrix,
- •  $M_3 = \mathbb{E}[(R - \mu)^{\otimes 3}]$  is the co-skewness tensor,
- •  $M_4 = \mathbb{E}[(R - \mu)^{\otimes 4}]$  is the co-kurtosis tensor.

## Optimization Problem

We maximize the Taylor-expanded expected utility subject to:

- • Dollar neutrality:  $\alpha^\top \mathbf{1} = 0$ ,
- • Unit characteristic exposure:  $\alpha^\top c = 1$ .

Thus, the optimization problem is:

$$\begin{aligned} \max_{\alpha} \quad & -\exp(-\lambda\bar{W}) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sigma_p^2 - \frac{\lambda^3}{6}s_p^3 + \frac{\lambda^4}{24}\kappa_p^4 \right] \\ \text{s.t.} \quad & \alpha^\top c = 1, \\ & \alpha^\top \mathbf{1} = 0. \end{aligned} \quad (11)$$

## Lagrangian Formulation

To solve this constrained maximization problem, we define the Lagrangian

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\alpha, \kappa, \theta) = & -\exp(-\lambda \bar{W}) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \alpha^\top M_2 \alpha - \frac{\lambda^3}{6} \alpha^\top M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha) + \frac{\lambda^4}{24} \alpha^\top M_4 (\alpha^{\otimes 3}) \right] \\ & - \kappa (\alpha^\top c - 1) - \theta (\alpha^\top \mathbf{1}), \end{aligned} \quad (12)$$

where  $\kappa$  enforces  $\alpha^\top c = 1$  and  $\theta$  enforces  $\alpha^\top \mathbf{1} = 0$ .

Using standard tensor derivatives:

$$\nabla_\alpha (\alpha^\top M_2 \alpha) = 2M_2 \alpha, \quad \nabla_\alpha [\alpha^\top M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha)] = 3M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha), \quad \nabla_\alpha [\alpha^\top M_4 (\alpha^{\otimes 3})] = 4M_4 (\alpha^{\otimes 3}),$$

the gradient of the Lagrangian is

$$\nabla_\alpha \mathcal{L} = -e^{-\lambda \bar{W}} \left[ \lambda^2 M_2 \alpha - \frac{\lambda^3}{2} M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha) + \frac{\lambda^4}{6} M_4 (\alpha^{\otimes 3}) \right] - \kappa c - \theta \mathbf{1}.$$

Setting  $\nabla_\alpha \mathcal{L} = 0$  and absorbing the exponential into the multipliers via

$$\tilde{\kappa} = \kappa e^{\lambda \bar{W}}, \quad \tilde{\theta} = \theta e^{\lambda \bar{W}},$$

We get

$$\lambda^2 M_2 \alpha - \frac{\lambda^3}{2} M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha) + \frac{\lambda^4}{6} M_4 (\alpha^{\otimes 3}) = \tilde{\kappa} c + \tilde{\theta} \mathbf{1}, \quad \alpha^\top c = 1, \quad \alpha^\top \mathbf{1} = 0. \quad (13)$$

# Higher-Order Utility Approximation with Shrinkage

## Lagrangian Formulation with Shrinkage

To solve the constrained maximization problem under CARA utility with higher-order moments and shrinkage, we define the Lagrangian:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\kappa}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = & -\exp(-\lambda \bar{W}) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top M_2 \boldsymbol{\alpha} - \frac{\lambda^3}{6} \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top M_3 (\boldsymbol{\alpha} \otimes \boldsymbol{\alpha}) + \frac{\lambda^4}{24} \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top M_4 (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\otimes 3}) \right] \\ & - \rho \|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|^2 - \kappa (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top c - 1) - \theta (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top \mathbf{1}), \end{aligned} \quad (14)$$

where

- •  $\rho > 0$  is the shrinkage parameter penalizing large portfolio weights,
- •  $\kappa$  enforces the characteristic exposure  $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top c = 1$ ,
- •  $\theta$  enforces dollar neutrality  $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top \mathbf{1} = 0$ .

Using standard tensor derivatives:

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top M_2 \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = 2M_2 \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \quad \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} [\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top M_3 (\boldsymbol{\alpha} \otimes \boldsymbol{\alpha})] = 3M_3 (\boldsymbol{\alpha} \otimes \boldsymbol{\alpha}), \quad \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} [\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top M_4 (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\otimes 3})] = 4M_4 (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\otimes 3}),$$

the gradient becomes:

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \mathcal{L} = -e^{-\lambda \bar{W}} \left[ \lambda^2 M_2 \boldsymbol{\alpha} - \frac{\lambda^3}{2} M_3 (\boldsymbol{\alpha} \otimes \boldsymbol{\alpha}) + \frac{\lambda^4}{6} M_4 (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\otimes 3}) \right] - 2\rho \boldsymbol{\alpha} - \kappa c - \theta \mathbf{1}.$$

Setting the gradient equal to zero and multiplying both sides by  $e^{\lambda \bar{W}}$ , define

$$\tilde{\kappa} = \kappa e^{\lambda \bar{W}}, \quad \tilde{\theta} = \theta e^{\lambda \bar{W}}.$$

$$\boxed{\lambda^2 M_2 \boldsymbol{\alpha} - \frac{\lambda^3}{2} M_3 (\boldsymbol{\alpha} \otimes \boldsymbol{\alpha}) + \frac{\lambda^4}{6} M_4 (\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\otimes 3}) + 2\rho e^{\lambda \bar{W}} \boldsymbol{\alpha} = \tilde{\theta} \mathbf{1} - \tilde{\kappa} c} \quad \text{with} \quad \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top c = 1, \boldsymbol{\alpha}^\top \mathbf{1} = 0.$$

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\alpha, \kappa, \theta) = & -\exp(-\lambda \bar{W}) \left[ 1 + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \alpha^\top M_2 \alpha - \frac{\lambda^3}{6} \alpha^\top M_3 (\alpha \otimes \alpha) \right] \\ & - \rho \|\alpha\|^2 - \kappa (\alpha^\top c - 1) - \theta (\alpha^\top \mathbf{1}), \end{aligned} \quad (16)$$

## B.2. Tokenomics Factor – Step-by-Step Explanation

### 1. Prepare the data:

Start by copying the main dataset and convert the date column (`datadate`) into a proper datetime format.

Keep only the relevant columns — the date (`datadate`), token identifier (`tic`), one-month return (`trt1m`), and token supply (`size`).

Rename these columns for readability:

`trt1m` → MonthlyReturn,    `size` → Supply.

### 2. Sort and calculate inflation (supply growth):

Sort the data by token (`tic`) and date (`datadate`) so that each token's history is in order.

Then, for each token, calculate the monthly inflation rate as the percentage change in supply from one month to the next:

$$\text{Inflation}_{i,t} = \frac{\text{Supply}_{i,t} - \text{Supply}_{i,t-1}}{\text{Supply}_{i,t-1}}.$$

This represents the rate at which each token's circulating supply grows — effectively, its inflation.

### 3. Rank inflation across tokens each month:

For every month, rank all tokens based on their inflation rate.

Tokens with higher inflation get higher ranks; tokens with lower inflation get lower ranks.

### 4. Center and normalize the ranks:

Compute  $N$ , the total number of unique tokens in the dataset.

Adjust each token's rank by subtracting  $(N+1)/2$  — this centers the ranks around zero.

Within each month, divide these centered ranks by their standard deviation to make them comparable across months.

Finally, multiply by  $-1$  so that tokens with higher inflation (less scarce) receive lower Tokenomics scores, and tokens with lower inflation (more scarce) receive higher scores:

$$\text{Tokenomics}_{i,t} = -\frac{\text{Rank}_{i,t} - (N+1)/2}{\text{Std}[\text{Rank}_t]}.$$

**5. Compute token-level contribution:**

Multiply each token's monthly return by its Tokenomics score:

$$\text{Component}_{i,t} = r_{i,t} \times \text{Tokenomics}_{i,t}.$$

This gives the contribution of each token to the Tokenomics factor for that month.

**6. Aggregate to form the monthly Tokenomics factor return:**

For each month, sum the token-level contributions across all tokens (after removing any missing data):

$$\text{TokenomicsFactorReturn}_t = \sum_i r_{i,t} \times \text{Tokenomics}_{i,t}.$$

The result is a single time series — the *Tokenomics Factor Return*, which represents the return of a portfolio that rewards tokens with slower supply growth and penalizes inflationary ones.
