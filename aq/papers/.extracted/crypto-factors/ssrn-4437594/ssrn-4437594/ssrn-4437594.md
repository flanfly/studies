**Abstract:** In this paper, we examine the performance of 49 cryptocurrency market anomalies in terms of their in-sample, out-of-sample, and full sample performance following the work of Liu et al. (2022). We find that, despite the similarities in anomaly performance between the in-sample and overall sample periods, there are noticeable changes in the behavior of out-of-sample anomalies. This includes the disappearance of size effect and the appearance of left-tail risk effect. Consequently, by using the Iterative Double Selection Lasso method, we construct a new threefactor model — DS3, which consists of the market factor (MKT), the two-week momentum factor (MOM2), and the residual momentum factor (RMOM). In comparison to CPT3 (Liu et al., 2022) and IPCA3 (Bianchi and Babiak, 2023), our DS3 model exhibits a certain advantage in explaining anomalies.

## **1. Introduction**

Cryptocurrencies are initially developed to address real-world transaction issues, such as reducing costs and tracking transactions (Nakamoto, 2008). However, their innovative nature and remarkable volatility transform them into an appealing investment and financing market. Since 2014, the cryptocurrency market witnesses rapid growth and extreme fluctuations, characterized by vast trading volumes, numerous ICOs and de-listings, and a diverse array of traders. Consequently, the cryptocurrency market evolves into a speculator's haven, where the prices of various currencies are subject to fluctuations influenced by factors like cryptocurrency issuance, the expansion of exchanges, and a diverse trader base. Investigating the factors that impact expected returns across the entire cryptocurrency market is an urgent area of study for both scholars and traders.

Scholars have conducted research on cross-sectional asset pricing in the cryptocurrency market and find a lot of anomalies. Liu et al. (2022) find significant momentum and size effects in their study from January 2014 to July 2020 and find size-related anomalies and momentum related anomalies show effective predictive power. Zhang et al. (2021) study the relationship between left-tail risk and expected returns from 2016 to 2020 and found that left-tail risk can positively predict expected returns cross-sectionally for this period. Cai and Zhao (2024) open up behavioral finance research on cross-sectional pricing in the cryptocurrency market by studying the salience effect. Dong et al. (2022) test cross-sectional anomalies in the cryptocurrency market at a monthly frequency from 2014 to 2019 and find that a decrease in liquidity enhances the performance of anomalies. Jiang et al. (2023) find the macroeconomic factors such as consumption, uncertainty, and sentiment play an important role in explaining the deviation from fundamentals in cryptocurrency.

In the realm of equities, as the number of anomalies has steadily grown, scholars have started leveraging machine learning techniques to tackle these high-dimensional anomalies (the "factor zoo", Harvey et al., 2016; Hou et al., 2020; Jensen et al., 2021) for more accurate predictions of asset returns (Gu et al., 2020a). Building upon this, some studies have used models with stronger generalization capabilities to capture the more intricate information within high-dimensional anomalies (Cong et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2020a), while others have resorted to "dimension reduction" methods or added penalty terms to linear models to extract the core factors that drive asset prices (Gu et al., 2020a; Kelly et al., 2019; Gu et al., 2020b; Freyberger et al. ,2020). Beyond prediction, another worthwhile application of machine learning methods is to more scientifically pick factors with potential explanatory power for returns from the vast array of pricing anomalies. Since factors often share economic and statistical correlations, research indicates that in high-dimensional settings, testing individual factors tends to produce biases (Belloni et al., 2012), and these biases can in turn affect the results of such factor tests. Meanwhile, the large model variance in high-dimensional scenarios makes it hard to carry out effective statistical inference. Feng et al. (2020) put forward that under the linear SDF framework, the Double-Selection Lasso can achieve asymptotically unbiased estimation of incremental factors within a highdimensional set of factors. Using machine learning methods to select effective pricing factors not only avoids the subjectivity of manual selection but also allows the chosen factors to better explain anomalies in financial markets, thereby forming an effective factor model.

In the realm of cryptocurrency, the exploration of factor models remains limited. Liu et al. (2022) devise factor combinations — CMKT, CSMB, and CMOM — providing rationale through a PCA (Principal Component Analysis) approach. Bianchi and Babiak (2023) suggest utilizing the IPCA (Instrumented Principal Component Analysis) method to formulate a latent factor model for the cryptocurrency market. Nevertheless, there is currently no universally accepted econometric methodology for constructing factor models that are specifically designed for the nuances of the cryptocurrency market. Feng et al. (2020) propose the Double-Selection Lasso to assess the contribution of new factors to existing factor models or factor libraries. The main issue addressed is how to select suitable factors from an existing factor library as explanatory variables to verify whether new factors have new sources of returns. Inspired by this, Chen et al. (2023) apply Iterative Double-Selection Lasso to construct a Global Factor Model, addressing stock asset pricing issues on a global scale.

In this study, we re-examine 49 anomalies in the cryptocurrency market and find that only 13 of them are significant from 2014 to 2023, which is consistent with Liu et al. (2022). However, the left-tail risk and salience group anomalies, which was respectively founded by Zhang et al. (2021) and Cai and Zhao (2024), are no longer significant during the full sample period. Additionally, our study employs Iterative Double-Selection Lasso to establish a factor model based on the anomalies constructed earlier. The DS3 model (which consists of the market factor, the two-week momentum factor, and the residual momentum factor) constructed in this study is compared with the cryptocurrency three-factor model proposed by Liu et al. (2022). The results indicate that, within the sample, our constructed DS3 model performs as well as Liu et al.'s (2022) factor model (referred to as CPT3 henceforth). However, out-of-sample, the model we constructed demonstrates even more superior performance. Furthermore, we investigated the factors contributing to the subpar performance of CPT3 over an extended period. Our study unveiled during out-of-sample period, CPT3 factors were ineffective in explaining left-tail risks and the size factor is not significant anymore. Integrating these insights with the outcomes of the Double-Selection method, we proposed the DS3 model, which, at its core, possesses the capability to comprehensively address all anomalies in the market. Notably, our factor model comparison also incorporates the IPCA3 model (a three-factor model built via IPCA), though IPCA is not inherently designed for explaining anomalies. The results indicate that while IPCA3 reduces anomaly magnitudes, it remains less effective than DS3 in explaining and eliminating them. At Last, we conduct robustness tests, which includes encompassing stability assessments of different anomalies through WLS-FM regression, the creation of anomalies based on cryptocurrencies with large market capitalization to evaluate factor model explanatory power, and adjustments to the factor construction method of CPT3 to alleviate the impact of grouping on factor explanatory power. These tests collectively demonstrate the robustness of our findings.

Our work makes two primary contributions to the literature on cryptocurrency market anomalies. First, we extend the work of Liu et al. (2022) by re-examining common anomalies in cryptocurrency markets using updated data, documenting significant changes in anomaly patterns - notably, the previously documented size effect appears to have diminished or disappeared entirely in more recent periods. Second, and more importantly, we develop a novel DS3 factor model constructed using Liu et al.'s original sample period data; crucially, this model demonstrates remarkable out-of-sample robustness as it continues to perform well when applied to post-Liu et al. periods, while also exhibiting superior anomaly-elimination capabilities compared to existing models. These findings suggest that our DS3 model represents a significant advancement in modeling cryptocurrency returns, offering both theoretical and practical improvements over current approaches.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we illustrate the data processing steps and the anomalies we construct as well as the principle of Double Selection Lasso (DS Lasso). In Section 3, we initially conduct single sorting tests based on different detection periods. Subsequently, we employ the DS-Lasso method to construct the DS3 factor model and compare its performance with CPT3. The fourth section is dedicated to discussing the underperformance of CPT3. In Section 5, significance tests were carried out. Section 6 provides a summary of the entire text.

#### **2.3 Selection model: Iterative double selection lasso**

According to Ross's (1976) APT model, the expected return of an asset can be expressed in the following form, where ��(���� �� ) represents the expected excess return of asset i, ���� represents the expected return of factor k, and ������ represents the exposure of asset i to factor k.

$$E(r_i^e) = u_i - r_f = \beta_{i1}f_1 + \beta_{i2}f_2 + \dots + \beta_{ik}f_k$$

If we want to assess the suitability of new factors within an existing factor model, the newly constructed factor model is:

$$E(r_i^e) = \alpha_i + \beta_h h_t + \beta_g g_t$$

Specifically, according to the derivation by Feng et al. (2020), the above content can be transformed into the following form:

$$E(r_t^e) = \ln\gamma_0 + C_h\lambda_h + C_g\lambda_g$$

Where  $C_h$  and  $C_g$  represent the covariances between factors and their expected returns,  $\lambda_h$  and  $\lambda_g$  represents the excess risk premium associated with the factors. In this context, factors enhance the explanatory capability of the existing factor set, akin to having substantial risk premiums. Given the potential size of the existing factor set, direct regression methods may result in high-dimensional econometric challenges. Consequently, Feng et al. (2020) ingeniously employ the Double Selection research framework to identify factors potentially correlated with those from the existing factor set  $h_t$ . These chosen factors are then integrated into the model, enabling the determination of the genuine excess risk premium of the testing factor  $g$ . The Double-Selection Lasso utilized in this study is an adaptation by Chen et al. (2023) aimed at addressing certain issues in the two-stage selection process. The specific steps are outlined as follows:

(Step 1a) Calculate the average return of assets in the sample, denoted as  $\bar{r}_t$ , using existing factors as explanatory variables and the average return as the dependent variable. Conduct the following Lasso regression to obtain factors with non-zero coefficients, collectively denoted as  $I_1$ . This step is undertaken to select factors with potential correlations to expected returns.

$$\bar{r}_t = \ln\gamma_0 + C_h\lambda_h$$

(Step 1b) Utilize existing factors as explanatory variables and the factor under examination as the dependent variable. Conduct Lasso regression to obtain factors with non-zero coefficients, denoted as  $I_2$ . Essentially, this step involves selecting factors from the existing set that are correlated with the factor under examination.

$$g_t = \alpha + \beta_h h_t + \epsilon$$

(Step 2) Compute the union of sets  $I_1$  and  $I_2$ , denoted as  $I_3$ . Employ the factors from  $I_3$  as control variables, with  $g$  as explanatory variables and as the  $\bar{r}_t$  dependent variable. Conduct an OLS regression to obtain coefficients of  $g$ , representing the risk premium, and simultaneously calculate the t-statistics for these coefficients.

$$\bar{r}_t = \ln\gamma_0 + C_{\{I_3\}}\lambda_{\{I_3\}} + C_g\lambda_g$$

Repeat the above process, with the initial set as the market factor. For each iteration, begin with a round of (Step 1a), followed by sequentially selecting anomalies from the 49 anomalies constructed in this study for (Step 1b) and (Step 2). Collect the risk premiums and t-statistics corresponding to each anomaly, select the

anomaly with the maximum t-statistic, and incorporate it into the formation of a new factor model. Continue this cycle until the risk premiums of the detected anomalies are not statistically significant. Due to the likelihood of having numerous factors in the final result, we adopt a stepwise regression method to further reduce the number of factors and obtain a new factor model. Initially, we eliminate variables with factor return t-statistics less than 1.64. Next, we conduct stepwise regression, choosing CAPM as the initial factor model. In each iteration, sequentially select factors from as the dependent variable, use the current model to explain them, obtain the risk premiums and t-statistics for each anomaly, and incorporate the anomaly with the maximum t-statistic into the factor model.

# **3. Empirical Results**

#### **3.1 Single sorting test**

Following Liu et al. (2022), we employ a single sorting test to construct portfolios based on 49 crosssectional features in the cryptocurrency market. These features are categorized into seven categories, namely size, momentum, trading volume, volatility, downside risk, left-tail risk, and salience. At the end of each week, we sort the cryptocurrencies into five groups based on the quantile of each characteristic and calculate the value-weighted returns for each portfolio. To construct the long-short portfolio, we take the difference between the highest and lowest groups. The Newey-West method is used to obtain adjusted *t*-values of the long-short portfolio return series, and we observe whether these return series are significantly different from zero. To enhance the examination of anomalies in the cryptocurrency market across diverse time frames, we independently assessed the performance of 49 anomalies during three distinct periods: January 2014 to July 2020, August 2020 to December 2023, and January 2014 to December 2023. The initial phase aligns with the period when Liu et al. (2022) identified anomalies and is denoted as the "in-sample period" in relation to our study. The subsequent phase is labeled the "out-of-sample period," and the third phase is termed the "fullsample period.

Table 3 presents the mean returns and t-test outcomes for both long and short combinations, formed utilizing 49 features across three distinct time frames. Notably, the peculiar performance of short-term momentum showcases remarkable consistency. Throughout the in-sample phase, one-week momentum, twoweek momentum, three-week momentum, four-week momentum, and one-to-three-week momentum all exhibit significance at the 5% level, yielding respective average weekly returns of 3.6% (t=2.635), 4.0% (t=3.779), 4.0% (t=3.831), 3.1% (t=3.065), and 2.6% (t=2.440). This implies that currencies demonstrating robust recent performance maintain positive trends in the future. Moreover, it is worth highlighting that this phenomenon persists in the remaining two periods. For example, in the out-of-sample phase, two-week momentum continues to generate a notable weekly return of 3.8% (t=4.943), and over the entire period, twoweek momentum sustains a return of 3.9% (t=5.322) per week. Surprisingly, residual momentum also exhibits substantial significance and consistency. In the in-sample period, the residual momentum anomaly yields an average weekly return of 2.1%, with a t-value of 2.422, signifying significance at the 5% level. Although it diminishes in the out-of-sample period, with returns decreasing to 1.2% per week and a corresponding t-value of 2.378, it remains statistically significant. Overall, across the entire sample, residual momentum averages a return of 1.8% (t=3.046), also significant at the 5% level. Regrettably, despite the significance and consistency of residual momentum across various sample periods, this analysis does not reveal a distinct superiority of residual momentum over traditional momentum. Similar conclusions have been evidenced in the context of the stock market, as Blitz et al. (2011) found that in the stock market, residual momentum demonstrates superior stability and risk-adjusted returns compared to traditional momentum.

Analyzing the characteristics of anomaly returns within and outside the sample, we uncover intriguing insights. Initially, anomalies associated with size exhibit significance in the in-sample phase. Notably, MACP, representing the logarithm of cryptocurrency market capitalization, demonstrates noteworthy returns in the constructed long-short portfolio, averaging -2.5% per week with a t-value of -3.711, signifying significance at the 1% level. Similarly, related anomalies such as PRC (price at the end of the last week), MAXPRC (maximum price during the last week), PRCVOL (based on trading volume in dollars), and STDVOLSCALED (standard deviation of trading volume in dollars) also manifest substantial returns during the in-sample period. The returns and t-values for portfolios built upon these anomalies are -2.4% (t=-2.088), -2.5% (t=-2.073), -2.4% (t=-2.495), and -2.7% (t=-3.025), respectively, all statistically significant at the 5% level. The consistent trend of these anomalies implies that smaller market cap currencies are likely undervalued or predisposed to yield higher returns in the subsequent week compared to their larger counterparts. However, intriguingly, during the out-of-sample period, these size-related anomalies lose their significance. For example, MACP only achieves a return of -0.1%, with a reduced t-value of -0.296, failing to reject the null hypothesis at the 5% significance level. Similarly, we observe returns and t-values of -0.9% (t=- 1.116) for PRC, -0.7% (t=-0.954) for MAXPRC, 0.3% (t=0.675) for PRCVOL, and -0.3% (t=-0.106) for STDVOLSCALED, none of which reach statistical significance at the 5% level. Nevertheless, overall, sizerelated anomalies persist across the entire sample, with MACP averaging a return of -1.7% and a t-value of - 3.617, signifying significance at the 5% level. The performance of other anomalies is comparable, and over an extended time period, the overall sample results align with the findings of Liu et al. (2022) during the backtesting interval (i.e., the in-sample period).

Additionally, during the out-of-sample period, anomalies measuring the absolute level of historical lefttail risk in the out-of-sample left-tail risk category and the SVAR (semi-variance) in downside risk become significant. Among these anomalies characterizing left-tail risk and left-skewed features, currencies with higher risk tend to be relatively overestimated, resulting in relatively smaller relative returns in the following week. According to our results, the maximum spread is observed in the MIN anomaly, with a performance of -2.5% (t=-3.990), and the spread is seen in the DES10 anomaly, with a performance of 0.1% (t=0.193). During this period, the performance of anomalies in the cryptocurrency market contradicts the findings of Zhang et al. (2021). They examined the cryptocurrency market from January 2016 to December 2020, calculated a 90 day value at risk (VaR), and found that VaR had a positive predictive ability for expected returns. This discrepancy may be due to differences in our calculation methods and calculation intervals. In the next section, we will test the cross-week predictive ability of anomalies, and the results of this test are consistent with Zhang et al. (2021). In the in-sample and full-sample periods, extreme risk and left-tail risk did not show significant predictive ability for expected returns. However, during the out-sample period, DVAR1 and DES1 could each achieve average weekly returns of 1.3% and 1.3%, respectively, and were statistically significant at the 5% level.

To summarize, it can be deduced that the pricing logic in the cryptocurrency market may have undergone substantial changes during the out-of-sample period. While the primary significant anomalies do not display inherent distinctions, the efficacy of the original factor model in pricing and elucidating anomalies might diminish after this timeframe. In light of this, we suggest conducting additional research to update the factor model.

## **3.2 Long-term exam**

In this subsection, we aim to test the long-term cross-sectional predictive ability of various characteristics on expected returns by constructing investment portfolios and calculating their returns over the future six weeks. Subsequently, we perform statistical inference on the time series of these returns. Our analysis aims to identify any anomalies that can predict cryptocurrency returns cross-sectionally and investor behavior over extended periods. If the previously significant characteristics remain significant in the test, it indicates that these anomalies can predict cryptocurrency returns cross-sectionally and investor behavior in the long term. If previously insignificant characteristics become significant in the test, we believe that these features can predict across periods. However, some mechanisms in the market may delay the predictive ability of these characteristics, possibly due to insufficient information transmission in the market.

In this section, we employ the Double-Selection Lasso methodology to construct a novel factor model based on the examined characteristics. Following the approach of Chen et al. (2023), we compute factor returns using a three-group sorting method. Specifically, we sort stocks into 30%-40%-30% groupings and calculate the value-weighted return spread between the top and bottom groups. To compare the factor model selected by Double Selection Lasso with the three-factor model constructed by Liu et al. (2022), we use data from January 2014 to July 2020 for factor selection with Double Selection Lasso and employ data from August 2020 to December 2023 as out-of-sample data to compare the out-of-sample explanatory power of different factor models. The final factor model (denoted as DS3 model) selected consists of MKT, MOM2, and RMOM. MKT represents the market value-weighted returns of all cryptocurrencies in the sample; MOM2 denotes the two-week momentum factor; RMOM is the residual momentum factor, calculated as the four-week cumulative residuals of regressing coin returns on the CAPM over the prior 4 weeks from week t-4 to week t. To eliminate the correlation between factors, we orthogonalize the factors following the approach of Liu et al. (2022)[<sup>2</sup>](#page-12-0) . Specifically, when constructing the RMOM factor, we first classify cryptocurrencies into winners and losers based on their returns over the past three weeks. Subsequently, within each group, we further divide the currencies into three parts based on the values of the RMOM feature—30%, 40%, and 30%, respectively. Finally, the residual momentum factor's returns are calculated using the following formula:

$$RMOM = \frac{1}{2}(\text{winner high} + \text{loser high}) - \frac{1}{2}(\text{winner low} + \text{loser low})$$

#### **3.4.1 In-sample test**

The adjusted absolute average return for anomalies stood at 1.950% (annualized at 101.400%), accompanied by an absolute average T-statistic value of 3.056 and a GRS of 0.000. Although slightly elevated compared to the in-sample scenario, the DS3 model showcased commendable outof-sample capabilities, taking into account both the magnitude of the T-statistic average (with a 5% significance threshold around 1.96) and comparisons among CAPM, CPT3 and IPCA3.

#### **3.4.4 Factor mutual explanations test**

In this section, following the approach of Liu et al. (2022) comparing CH3 and FF3 factors, we investigate CPT3, DS3 and IPCA3 by mutually explaining their unique factors. Meanwhile, the adjusted alphas for the three IPCA factors are 4.697% (t = 0.103), 1.742 (t = 0.688), and 3.564 (t = 1.989). Except for the last factor, the DS3 model successfully explains the other two IPCA3 factors. Additionally, in the GRS test for the joint alpha of CSMB and CMOM being zero, the p-value is 0.897, indicating that, at the 5% significance level, we cannot reject the null hypothesis. The same applies to the IPCA3 model. Under the explanation of the DS3 model, the three factors of the IPCA3 model yield a GRS statistic of 1.841 with a p-value of 0.139. Therefore, we conclude that the DS3 model can effectively explain the information contained in CPT3 and IPCA3.

In contrast, CPT3 fails to effectively explain the factors in the DS3 model. After controlling for the CPT3 model, RMOM retains an alpha of 89.4 basis points (annualized at 46.88%) with a t-statistic of 2.579, which is statistically significant at the 5% level. Similarly, IPCA3 also fails to fully account for the DS3 factors. After adjusting for the IPCA3 model, RMOM still exhibits an alpha of 95 basis points with a t-statistic of 2.471, significant at the 5% level, while MOM2 shows an alpha of 255 basis points with a highly significant t-statistic of 2.553 (5% level). The GRS test p-value of 0.001 leads to rejection of the null hypothesis at the 5% level, confirming that DS3 contains pricing information beyond what IPCA3 can explain.

In summary, both CPT3 and IPCA3 are unable to fully explain the factor structure embedded in the DS3 model, highlighting the incremental explanatory power of DS3.

## **4. Further Analysis:**

#### **4.1 Why is there a decline in the explanatory power of the CPT3 model?**

In the preceding section, we established that the CPT3 model adeptly elucidates anomalies within the insample data and demonstrates satisfactory performance. Nevertheless, in the out-of-sample phase, its explanatory efficacy experiences a discernible downturn. The average absolute value of the t-test statistic for interpreting its alpha is 2.075, notably surpassing the 5% significance threshold of 1.96. In this section, we examine the performance of both CPT3 and DS3 across different stages to elucidate the factors contributing to the subpar performance of CPT3 in the out-of-sample segment.

Firstly, it is noteworthy that, in contrast to Liu et al. (2022), the returns of MACP anomalies remain statistically significant even after undergoing explanation by CPT3, registering a weekly rate of 1.5% during the in-sample phase (t=3.117). Concurrently, a considerable decline is observed in the t-statistics of PRC and MAXPRC, dropping from 2.088 to 0.712 and from 2.353 to 0.816, respectively. This implies that the 30-40- 30 grouping approach used for the CSMB factor in CPT3 does not fully capture market value anomalies. A similar trend is evident across the entire sample period, where the returns of MACP, post-CPT3 explanation, achieve 1.0% (t=2.971). However, there is a significant reduction in the significance levels of PRCVOL and STDPRCVOL throughout the entire sample period. Furthermore, following Size-neutralization, CMOM shows a notable decrease in its explanatory capacity. In the in-sample phase, it falls short in explaining twoweek momentum, with the alpha for two-week momentum at 1.6%. Despite a reduction in absolute magnitude, the t-value remains significant at 1.821 at the 10% significance level. Additionally, the CMOM factor does not exhibit a strong explanatory ability for two-week momentum in both the out-of-sample phase and the entire sample period.

We identify an additional concern in the CPT3 factor model, specifically its capacity to elevate initially insignificant anomalies to a significant level. Notably, in the realm of downside risk and tail risk, anomalies associated with Value-at-risk and expected-shortfall lacked significance before the CPT3 adjustment, with absolute value returns spanning from 0.07% to 1.6% (in-sample). However, following the CPT3 test, anomaly returns consistently achieved significance, with weekly returns surpassing 1.0%, and absolute t-values ranging from a minimum of 0.837 to a maximum of 3.103. Over the entire sample duration, the absolute values of downside risk and left-tail risk also manifested corresponding features.

To sum up, the lackluster performance of CPT3 in the out-of-sample and entire sample periods can be attributed to two main factors. Firstly, the factor model encounters difficulty in elucidating anomalies directly associated with the factor logic. This challenge may stem from differences in the grouping methodology, hinting at a potential resolution through adjustments to the grouping approach. Secondly, previously insignificant anomalies gain a level of significance after being interpreted by the factor model. Despite these anomalies falling outside the scope of those identified in Table 6, the emergence of noteworthy returns following factor model interpretation aligns with the statistical definition of anomalies.

To test the robustness of the single sorting test results, we employ the value-weighted Fama-MacBeth regression method. In accordance with Liu et al. (2022), we begin by sorting the features in ascending order and dividing the cryptocurrencies into five portfolios. We then replace the characteristic value in each portfolio with a corresponding rank from 1 to 5. This approach is useful in mitigating the impact of extreme values that are frequently observed in the cryptocurrency market, while also ensuring the relative size of the features remains consistent. This methodology aids in analyzing the linear correlation between each characteristic and expected return, which enables us to evaluate the cross-sectional predictive power of each feature on expected returns.

#### **6. Conclusion**

In this investigation, we reassessed the performance of anomalies in the cryptocurrency market, drawing upon the research by Liu et al. (2022). Our examination covered their behavior across the in-sample, out-ofsample, and overall sample periods. Notably, we observed that while significant anomalies identified in the in-sample period largely retained their significance in the overall sample, the market value effect diminished in significance during the out-of-sample phase. Additionally, the predictive influence of left-tail risk on expected returns shifted from positive to negative, signaling substantial shifts in the cryptocurrency market during the out-of-sample period. This implies a potential need for a re-evaluation of factor models constructed based on earlier logic.

Moreover, by employing the Double Selection Lasso method, we crafted the cryptocurrency three-factor model (DS3) using in-sample data. Our DS3 model demonstrated a discernible advantage in elucidating anomalies in both the out-of-sample and overall sample periods when compared to Liu et al. (2022). We also compare the DS3 factor model with the IPCA3 factor model. The results show that while the IPCA3 model can reduce the scale of anomalies, the DS3 model demonstrates superior performance in eliminating the statistical significance of anomalies. Furthermore, factor spanning tests reveal that the DS3 model effectively incorporates information from both the CPT3 and IPCA3 models. We also conducted a concise analysis to understand the reasons behind the underperformance of the CPT3 model. Lastly, our results underwent a battery of significance tests, encompassing stability assessments of different anomalies through WLS-FM regression, the creation of anomalies based on cryptocurrencies with large market capitalization to evaluate factor model explanatory power, and adjustments to the factor construction method of CPT3 to alleviate the impact of grouping on factor explanatory power, among other tests.

In summary, our findings affirm that the factor model constructed using DS-Lasso exhibits a certain superiority. From a historical standpoint, its explanatory capacity outperforms that of CPT3 and IPCA3.
