Category: **comparison** — the paper runs horse races between cryptocurrency-specific and equity risk factors in cross-sectional Fama-MacBeth regressions over the whole large-cap cryptocurrency market.

### Claim

The paper asks which risk factors — cryptocurrency-specific or equity — carry risk premiums in the cross-section of cryptocurrencies with market capitalisation above $1 million (1,986 coins, weekly observations, January 6, 2014 – November 1, 2020). Using a two-step Fama-MacBeth procedure with 26-week rolling beta estimation windows, the authors find that only three factors are priced consistently with significant positive premiums: equity downside market risk, cryptocurrency size (CSMB), and cryptocurrency policy uncertainty (UCRY). The equity market factor premium is 13% p.a. with t-stat 2.12 (Table 4, Panel A col. 1); the equity downside-risk premium is 0.10–0.18 p.a. with t-stats 2.15–2.43 (Table 4 col. 2, Table 5 cols. 2–5); CSMB premiums range 0.35–0.41 with t-stats 2.04–2.31 (Tables 4–5); and the UCRY premium is 0.03 p.a. with t-stats 2.08–2.29 (Tables 4–5). Equity downside risk displaces cryptocurrency downside risk when both enter jointly (Table 5), which the authors read as evidence that cryptocurrency–equity market integration increased over the sample, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic. Momentum is not priced in crypto: CWML earned an insignificant average 0.46 p.a. [0.95] and negative premiums in the cross-section (Tables 3–4), and equity WML premiums are negative in every region tested (Table 6). The intercepts remain large and highly significant (1.39–1.84 across all reported specifications) with model R² no higher than 0.14, so most average crypto returns remain unexplained.

### Fit with our constraints

Instrument class is spot cryptocurrency and, for the equity legs, standard equity factor portfolios. The crypto factors (CMKT, CSMB, CWML) are long–short zero-cost cap-weighted portfolios of coins; both legs are expressible in crypto spot or perpetuals (shorting a cap-weighted 30% tail of the crypto cross-section requires per-coin perps or spot shorts; the paper does not discuss futures or perps). Long and short legs are never reported separately — every factor is a net long–short spread. No leverage is used or mentioned; factors are zero-cost by construction. Replication needs: coinmarketcap.com daily prices and capitalisations including delisted coins, Kenneth French data library equity factors, CBOE VIX, VCRIX from thecrix.de, and UCRY from Brian Lucey's website. Weekly frequency (Monday open to Friday close) keeps crypto returns comparable to equity weekly returns. No transaction costs, financing costs, or capacity figures are reported anywhere in the paper; all returns and premiums are gross.

### Strategies

The paper's factors are its units. All are tested in the shared setup described under Test setup; the differences are the signal definitions and construction rules below.

**CMKT (cryptocurrency market factor).** The capitalisation-weighted portfolio of all cryptocurrencies in the sample (§2). It is not a long–short spread. Its mean annualised return is 0.88 [3.41], Sharpe 0.18, weekly standard deviation 4.86, skewness 0.52, kurtosis 2.41 (Table 2). Its premium in cross-sectional regressions is 0.13 [0.63] alone (Table 4 Panel B col. 1) and insignificant in all other specifications; the authors frame the insignificance as a consequence of the factor's high volatility (footnote 5). Bitcoin is tested as an alternative market proxy: its premium averages 0.19, the time series of the Bitcoin and CMKT premiums correlate at 0.91, and results are "all similar" (footnote 5, not tabulated).

**CSMB (cryptocurrency size factor).** Weekly-rebalanced zero-cost portfolio: long the 30% smallest and short the 30% biggest cryptocurrencies, capitalisation-weighted (§2, following Liu et al. 2020). Mean annualised return 0.61** [2.26], Sharpe 0.12, positive skewness 1.77 (Table 3). The authors attribute the outsized returns to occasional very high returns of small coins. Priced cross-sectionally with premiums 0.35** [2.04] and 0.41** [2.21] (Table 4 Panel B cols. 3–4) and 0.35**–0.40** in Table 5; significance weakens to 0.26 [1.63] when UCRY is added (Table 4 col. 7). The paper frames size as a priced crypto-specific factor; it does not commit to a risk-based versus behavioural mechanism.

**CWML (cryptocurrency momentum factor).** Weekly-rebalanced zero-cost portfolio: long the 30% of coins with the highest previous-week returns, short the 30% with the lowest, capitalisation-weighted. The sort uses a one-week horizon, explicitly chosen over Liu et al.'s (2020) three-week horizon "because it generates the highest momentum in our sample period"; longer horizons leave results "not affected significantly" but the effect is less pronounced (footnote 2). Mean annualised return 0.46 [0.95] — economically large, statistically insignificant (Table 3). Cross-sectional premiums are negative but insignificant: -0.18 [-0.48] and -0.21 [-0.58] (Table 4 Panel B cols. 3–4). The authors attribute the collapse to extreme positive returns of a few past-loser coins in 2019–2020; the average momentum return was significantly higher in 2014–2018 (footnote 4). Skewness -0.85 and kurtosis 9.43, cited as evidence of momentum-crash risk (Table 3). Equity and crypto momentum factors are uncorrelated (-0.05, t -0.85, Table 3).

**Equity downside market risk (MKT-).** Downside beta is estimated in the two-beta CAPM regression (Ang et al. 2006): $R_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i^- R_{MKT,t} + \gamma_i R_{MKT,t} dummy_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}$ (eq. 2), where $dummy_t = 0$ if $R_{MKT,t} < 0$ and 1 otherwise, so $\beta_i^-$ is the market beta conditional on negative market returns. In the second step the extra downside beta enters as $(\beta_i^- - \beta_i)\lambda^-$ (eq. 4, the Lettau et al. 2014 downside-risk CAPM). The equity downside premium is 0.10** [2.43] (Table 4 Panel A col. 2), 0.17** [2.15], 0.18** [2.30] and 0.12** [2.33] (Table 5 cols. 2, 4, 5). The crypto-market downside premium (0.28** [2.56], 0.31*** [2.58], Table 4 Panel B cols. 2–3) becomes insignificant (0.03–0.05) once equity downside risk enters jointly (Table 5). The paper's framing is risk-based: coins with higher exposure to market downside are compensated by higher subsequent returns.

**UCRY (cryptocurrency uncertainty factor).** Taken from Lucey et al. (2021), data retrieved from Brian Lucey's website (§2). Lucey et al. construct two indices — price uncertainty and policy uncertainty; the paper tests both and reports that only policy uncertainty is priced, with a robust premium of 0.03** per annum (t-stats 2.12, 2.29, 2.23 in Table 4 Panel B cols. 5–7; 2.08 in Table 5 col. 5). The price-uncertainty premium is "insignificant in all specifications and not reported" (footnote 7). The paper does not describe the index construction — only the source. The authors interpret the policy-uncertainty result as indirect evidence of institutional investors in crypto markets (since Lucey et al. argue informed investors are sensitive to policy uncertainty).

**VCRIX and VIX (volatility factors).** VCRIX (Kim et al. 2019, from thecrix.de) is a model-based forecast of the next-30-days volatility of the CRIX index, built to parallel VIX; it is normalised to VIX's starting value on 5.12.2014 (§3). The two implied-volatility series correlate 0.20, statistically significant, with simultaneous spikes at the March 2020 COVID outbreak (§3). Premiums are positive but only significant at the 90% level: VIX 20.55* [1.73] (Table 4 Panel A col. 6), VCRIX 12.38 [1.63] (Table 4 Panel B col. 8). Note the point estimates are in volatility-index units, not return units, so their magnitudes are not comparable to other premiums; the paper reports them as-is.

**Equity factors (MKT, SMB, HML, WML, CMA, RMW, VIX) and regional variants.** Collected from the Kenneth French data library for the US, four regions (Asia-Pacific, North America, Europe, Japan) and the global market; daily data converted to weekly where weekly is unavailable (§2). In the US four-factor Carhart and five-factor Fama-French specifications, none of SMB, HML, CMA, RMW is priced: premiums range -0.01 to 0.37, all t-stats below 1.74 in magnitude (Table 4 Panel A cols. 3–4). US WML premium is -0.10* [-1.72] (Table 4 col. 3). In the regional 4-factor models (Table 6), WML premiums are significantly negative everywhere: global -0.09** [-2.12], North American -0.09* [-1.92], European -0.10** [-2.22], Asian-Pacific -0.05** [-2.14], Japanese -0.12*** [-2.62], which the authors find puzzling given the crypto momentum factor is also negative-correlated with equity momentum. The global market premium is 0.11** [2.10] — the highest among regions — so global and US indices are judged the best market proxies; the weakest relation is with Asia-Pacific (footnote 3: CMKT correlation 0.08, beta 0.35 there, versus European correlation 0.16, beta 0.63).

**What the comparison establishes.** In joint specifications (Table 5) equity market and equity downside risk dominate their crypto counterparts, while CSMB and UCRY stay significant after controlling for equity factors; the model with equity downside risk, CSMB and UCRY has the highest R² (0.14, Table 5 col. 5). The paper concludes crypto's factor structure is real but weak — "only emerging" — with large unexplained alphas.

### Test setup

Sample period is the full available history with a usable cross-section: January 6, 2014 – November 1, 2020 (356 weeks, 216,369 return observations, 1,986 cryptocurrencies, up to ~1,900 traded simultaneously — Table 1, Table 4 footnote). Weekly returns are computed from Monday opening prices to Friday closing prices. Universe screens: market capitalisation above $1 million (coins enter on crossing and may leave on dropping below), plus at least 27 weeks of uninterrupted observations. The data are from coinmarketcap.com and include delisted cryptocurrencies, so "there is no concern about survivorship bias" (§2); delisting-return handling beyond inclusion is not described. The data are filtered by excluding 0.005% of outliers; even so, remaining weekly returns range from -100% to 7,189% (Table 1). Fama-MacBeth: betas are estimated in rolling 26-week (half-year) windows from eqs. (1)–(2), the window advances one week, and the subsequent week's cross-section of returns is regressed on the trailing betas in eqs. (3)–(4), producing time-series of weekly premiums whose averages are reported annualised in Tables 4–6. So the signal-to-trade gap is at most one week after the beta-estimation window closes. No out-of-sample or post-publication period is reported. All reported premiums are gross; no transaction-cost, borrow-cost, or financing adjustments appear anywhere.

### Robustness

There is no section labelled robustness. Sensitivity checks are embedded: Bitcoin instead of CMKT as the market proxy (footnote 5: results similar, not tabulated, premium 0.19); one- versus three-week momentum sorting horizons (footnote 2: longer horizons less pronounced); 0.005% outlier exclusion; five regional equity-factor sets (Table 6); and price- versus policy-uncertainty indices (footnote 7). Subperiod evidence appears only narratively: momentum was significantly positive in 2014–2018 and lost significance in 2019–2020 (footnote 4), and the equity linkage strengthened in the COVID-19 period (Section 5 discussion).

### Specification search

The paper reports 24 Fama-MacBeth specifications in total: 6 equity-factor and 8 crypto-factor columns in Table 4, 5 joint horse-race columns in Table 5, and 5 regional columns in Table 6. It does not report results for all variants examined: the Bitcoin-proxy regressions, the price-uncertainty index, and the longer momentum-horizon sorts are mentioned as tested but not tabulated.

### Limitations and future work

The authors' stated limitations: the crypto factor structure is only emerging, average unexplained returns (alphas) remain high, and the significant negative equity-momentum premiums are unexplained and flagged for "further investigation." Future work they propose: exploring other factors from various asset markets or new crypto-specific factors for returns and volatility, and building a single asset-pricing model that prices crypto returns together with other asset classes, since the stochastic discount factor is common across markets.

### Separable variants for our pipeline

For this comparison paper, the list is largely the strategies again; each factor is independently implementable.

- CSMB long–short size factor (30%/30% cap-weighted tails, weekly rebalance, 1-week signal lag) — §2, Table 3.
- CWML long–short momentum factor (one-week prior return sort, weekly rebalance) — §2, Table 3; the 3-week-horizon variant noted in footnote 2 is a second, distinct variant.
- Crypto downside-beta factor (26-week rolling two-beta regression, eq. 2) — §4.
- UCRY policy-uncertainty exposure as a conditioning variable — §4, footnote 7 (construction not described in this paper).
- VCRIX volatility exposure — §3–4.
- Equity downside beta of the crypto cross-section as a cross-sectional signal — Table 4 col. 2, Table 5.
