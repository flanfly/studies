**Category: modification.** The baseline is the Fama-French (1993) three-factor model; the thesis re-implements it per the published instructions on a new universe (S&P Composite 1500) at two frequencies and then modifies it by adding the Bitcoin return as a fourth factor in a log-log specification. The unit of interest is the delta: baseline three-factor model versus three-factor-plus-Bitcoin. The baseline is described precisely in the thesis (§3.2, §4.2) and is the FF 1993 construction as published, with two deviations the thesis acknowledges: breakpoints are the median and 30th/70th percentiles of S&P Composite 1500 stocks (not NYSE), and firms with non-December fiscal years are retained (§4.2).

### Claim

Applying the Fama-French 1993 three-factor model to six value-weighted size/book-to-market portfolios from the S&P Composite 1500 (nonfinancial, nonutility firms with positive book equity), the thesis finds the market, size (SMB), and value (HML) factors jointly explain portfolio excess returns with adjusted R² of 79.58% on monthly data (July 2003–December 2022) and 71.15% on daily data (July 2018–December 2022), with all factor coefficients statistically significant and GRS tests unable to reject zero alphas (monthly p-values 0.62, 0.58, 0.74, 0.65 for one-, two- (two variants), and three-factor models; §5.1.4). On 29 blockchain- or cryptocurrency-related firms from the same index, adding the Bitcoin return as a fourth factor (in logs) collapses adjusted R² to 19.31%, makes the log-HML coefficient insignificant, and yields a significant positive coefficient of 0.13 on log Bitcoin return, which the authors interpret as Bitcoin acting as a diversifier for these stock portfolios (§5.3.3, §6.1). The thesis thereby "proves" the Fama-French (1993) results and, per the GRS test, judges the three-factor model superior to the four-factor version (§5.3.2).

### Fit with our constraints

Instrument classes are US equities (large-, mid-, and small-cap, S&P Composite 1500) and Bitcoin spot returns. The paper never trades anything; it regresses portfolio excess returns on factors. The SMB and HML factors are constructed as long-short spreads (small-minus-big, high-minus-low), but long and short legs are never reported separately — only factor spread series and portfolio excess returns — so no short-leg tradability question is resolved by the paper itself. No leverage is used anywhere, and no transaction costs, financing costs, or turnover are described; all returns are gross (thesis does not state this caveat; it simply never mentions costs). Replication would need: Refinitiv Eikon market and accounting data (January 2002–December 2022), Bitcoin daily closing prices from coinmarketcap.com (July 2018–December 2022), 10-K filings from SEC EDGAR for the blockchain-firm screen, the Kenneth R. French database for comparison factors, and Stata for all construction and tests (§4.1). The approximate point-in-time protection is the standard six-month gap between fiscal year-end accounting data and portfolio returns, with the caveat that non-December fiscal year-end firms are retained in the sample (§4.2). Survivorship and delisting handling are not addressed.

### Strategies

**Baseline — the FF1993 three-factor model on the S&P Composite 1500.** Signal: the three Fama-French factors, constructed exactly as follows (§4.2). At the end of each June of year *t*, stocks are ranked on size (market capitalization = closing price × shares, June of year *t*); the median of S&P Composite 1500 stocks splits small/big. BE/ME is book common equity for the fiscal year ending in calendar year *t*−1 (stockholders' equity plus balance-sheet deferred taxes and investment tax credit minus preferred stock value, per FF 1993; following the Kenneth R. French website update the thesis excludes deferred taxes and investment tax credits, and uses redemption value for preferred stock if available) divided by market equity at end of December of *t*−1. Breakpoints for BE/ME are the 30th and 70th percentiles, giving six value-weighted portfolios (small/big × low/medium/high). Negative-BE firms are excluded; firms with non-December fiscal years are kept (dropping them would leave too small a sample, §4.2). All variables are winsorized at the 5th and 95th percentiles. Returns are continuously compounded, `R_it = 100 × [ln(P_it) − ln(P_it−1)]` (eq. 4.1), from closing prices already adjusted for dividends and splits, so no dividend adjustment is needed. The risk-free rate is the one-month US Treasury bill rate (Refinitiv RIC "US1MFRB=RR"). The factors are `SMB_B/M = ((S/L)+(S/M)+(S/H))/3 − ((B/L)+(B/M)+(B/H))/3` (eq. 4.3) and, as rendered in the paper (eq. 4.4, garbled in extraction; the text describes it as high-minus-low averaged across size groups), `HML = (S/H+B/H)/2 − (S/L+B/L)/2`, each built from value-weighted portfolio returns. Portfolios are reformed annually at end of June; monthly data give 234 observations, daily data 1,134. Firm count after screens grows from 662 (2003) to 1,001 (2022); average annual per-portfolio firm counts run 720–782 with average annual market caps of 1,240–49,500 million USD and average BE/ME from 0.11 (big-low) to 1.34 (small-high) (Tables 1–2). The authors frame size and value as risk-based (dividend discount model relation in eq. 3.4; Fama-French 1993). They do not name the other side of these factor trades. Time-series OLS per portfolio (eq. 3.2) gives adjusted R² of 93.13%–96.38% (monthly, Table 5) and 93.01%–95.43% (daily, Table 9); the market factor has the largest coefficients (monthly 0.98 small / 1.09 big; daily 1.03 / 1.14), SMB is positive in small and negative in big portfolios (insignificant only in big-high), and HML rises from low to high BE/ME (insignificant in the two medium portfolios). Pooled OLS (POLS, chosen over FE/RE via Breusch-Pagan Lagrangian multiplier test, p=1.00) yields monthly R² 0.7958 with coefficients (market, SMB, HML) = (1.16, 0.50, 0.13) and a significant intercept of −0.00175 (§5.1.5), and daily R² 0.7115 with (1.30, 0.52, 0.13) and an insignificant intercept (§5.2.5). Execution timing: portfolios formed at June-end, a six-month lag between fiscal year-end accounting data and test returns; intra-holding-period rebalancing frequency, turnover, and any trade-date lag are not specified. Returns in the daily panels are contemporaneous; the thesis assumes synchronous monthly/daily observation.

**Modified — the three-factor model plus Bitcoin return.** The delta is the addition of the fourth factor and a change of specification: everything is log-transformed, `log(R_it − R_ft) = α_i + β_i log(R_mt − R_ft) + s_i log(SMB_t) + h_i log(HML_t) + b_i log(R_bitcoin,t) + ε_it` (eq. 4.6), justified by the authors as linearising the nonlinear Bitcoin-stock relation (following Gil-Alana et al. 2020 and Cahill et al. 2020; §4.2). Bitcoin return is `R_bitcoin,t = 100 × [ln(P_bitcoin,t) − ln(P_bitcoin,t−1)]` (eq. 4.5) from daily closing prices. The universe is the 29 S&P Composite 1500 firms whose 10-K filings contain the keywords "Bitcoin", "Cryptocurrency", or "Blockchain" (search via SEC EDGAR, not 8-K, §4.1; ticker list in Appendix C, Table C1); daily data July 2018–December 2022. POLS (Breusch-Pagan p > 0.05) gives adjusted R² of 19.31% (R² 0.2018) on 373 observations (Table F3), with coefficients (log market, log SMB, log HML, log Bitcoin, constant) = (0.37, 0.21, −0.05, 0.13, −1.30); log HML is insignificant (t = −0.80, p = 0.423), the other three significant (t = 7.48, 3.98, 3.05); FGLS leaves coefficients unchanged (Table F4). The number of observations drops from 6,804 (full daily panel) to 373 for the log-log model; the paper does not acknowledge or explain this loss, though the log of any non-positive value is undefined. The GRS test on the four-factor model returns a mean absolute alpha "greater than zero and statistically significant", i.e., the four-factor model leaves abnormal returns; the three-factor model is judged better (§5.3.2). Dropping HML changes coefficients to (0.41, 0.10, 0.11) with adjusted R² 0.1917 on 703 observations — a 0.14% reduction — so HML is called "practically ineffective" (§5.3.3; Tables F10–F11). For comparison, the plain three-factor model on just the 29 firms (no Bitcoin, no logs) has adjusted R² 0.2739 with significant positive coefficients (1.41, 0.25, 0.13) and an insignificant intercept (§5.3.4; Tables F14–F15). A positive, weak Bitcoin coefficient (0.13) is interpreted as diversification (per Huang et al. 2021, Chan et al. 2019); the thesis positions its results closer to Cheng et al. (2019) (significant positive Bitcoin coefficients 0.04 and 0.09) than to Cahill et al. (2020) (positive but insignificant).

### Test setup

Sample periods: July 2003–December 2022 monthly (234 months), and July 2018–December 2022 daily (1,134 days); the twenty-year monthly window follows the convention of asset-pricing studies needing ≥20 years (citing Cochrane 2005), while the daily window compensates for the shorter Bitcoin-availability period and the thin pre-2018 population of blockchain firms (§1). Data sources by name: Refinitiv Eikon (market and accounting data, risk-free rate), www.coinmarketcap.com (Bitcoin prices), SEC EDGAR (10-K filings), Kenneth R. French database (comparison factors), Stata (all processing) (§4.1). Survivorship and delisting are not addressed. All reported figures are gross of costs, which are never modelled. Benchmarking: the constructed factors correlate with the Kenneth R. French factors at 79.47% (SMB), 80.34% (HML), 99.60% (market) monthly and 78.69%, 83.66%, 99.62% daily (§5.1.1, §5.2.1), and the same regressions re-run on Kenneth R. French data give similar results (adjusted R² 89.17%–95.35% monthly, Table 6; 90.75%–98.17% daily, Table 10).

### Robustness

What the authors tested: comparison against Kenneth R. French database factors (correlations and re-estimation of all portfolio regressions on French's data, yielding broadly similar loadings and R², Tables 6, 10, D6, E6); monthly versus daily frequency; GRS tests across one-, two-, and three-factor models (monthly p-values 0.62/0.58/0.74/0.65; daily relies on mean absolute alpha because the Stata grsftest command fails above 800 observations); a battery of diagnostics per portfolio and for the panels — ADF and Levin-Lin-Chu unit-root tests (all stationary), VIF (all < 10), Durbin-Watson (monthly: negative autocorrelation in all portfolios; daily: positive autocorrelation in all), Breusch-Pagan (monthly: heteroskedasticity only in big-high; daily: only in small-high), Jarque-Bera (mixed normality), Wooldridge panel autocorrelation (p = 0.38 monthly, 0.07 daily), with FGLS re-estimation correcting heteroskedasticity/autocorrelation without changing coefficients; and removal of HML from the four-factor model (§5.1.6, §5.2.6, §5.3.4, Appendix D–F). The thesis explicitly attributes the R² collapse in the Bitcoin stage to the 29-firm sample, crypto-specific volatility, and COVID-19 covering a fifth of the period (§5.3.4). There is no subperiod analysis, no alternative breakpoints or weighting (equal weighting is mentioned and rejected in favour of value weighting), no delayed-execution test, and no cost test.

### Specification search

The thesis reports the 2×3 factor construction implemented; the alternative 2×2 construction is described (§3.2) but not implemented. Reported model variants: 6 portfolio time-series regressions × (monthly, daily, KRF-monthly, KRF-daily); GRS on one-, two (two variants)-, and three-factor models; panel POLS/FGLS monthly and daily; the log-log four-factor model with and without HML; and the plain three-factor daily model on the 29-firm subsample. One winsorisation scheme (5th/95th percentiles), one sort-rebalance scheme, and no parameter grids are examined; results are reported for all fitted specifications in the appendices.

### Limitations and future work

The authors' own: (i) effects of major events (2008–09 financial crisis, COVID-19 pandemic) were not excluded, though prior work shows R² and coefficients change across such events; (ii) the cryptocurrency market is young and behaves differently from mature equity markets, so results may change as adoption grows; (iii) only 29 blockchain firms were available, and small samples weaken inference; no remedy is offered. Suggested future work: repeat with another US or non-US reference market to compare, and use cryptocurrencies other than Bitcoin (Ethereum, Tether, Binance Coin) (§6.2).

### Separable variants for our pipeline

This being a modification paper, the list is largely the strategies again: (1) the FF1993 three-factor construction (SMB, HML, market premium per §3.2/§4.2, eqs. 4.3–4.4); (2) the log-log four-factor specification with Bitcoin as the added factor (eq. 4.6); (3) the three-factor-minus-HML variant on the 29 blockchain firms (§5.3.3); (4) the 29-firm blockchain-stock universe itself (Appendix C, Table C1), which is a ready-made crypto-exposure screen from 10-K keyword search (§4.1).
