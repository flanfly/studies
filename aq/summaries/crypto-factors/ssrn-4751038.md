### Claim

This is a systematic literature review, not an empirical study: it conducts no estimation of its own. The paper's claim is that the peer-reviewed literature on cryptocurrency pricing, synthesised from 88 articles, identifies influential factors that fall into six categories — supply and demand, technology, economics, market volatility, investors' attributes, and social media — with the categories not mutually exclusive (Results; Discussion). The review's own headline finding is descriptive: Bitcoin is the most studied currency, the field is emerging (earliest article 2014, most articles published in 2022), and the most common methods in the reviewed literature are the vector autoregression model (9 studies) and the autoregressive distributed lag model (6 studies) (Research methods; Discussion). No single effect, magnitude, t-statistic, or return figure is reported anywhere in the paper, because none is computed; every quantitative statement about factor direction is attributed to an underlying study. The paper was received 26 May 2023, revised 9 August 2023, accepted 31 August 2023 (page 1); the literature search was conducted in August 2022 (Method) — the Discussion says the review covers articles "published before September 2022" (Discussion), an internal discrepancy the paper does not reconcile.

### Fit with our constraints

No strategy, signal, or backtest is proposed, so the mechanical checks (short legs, leverage, turnover, gross-vs-net) do not apply to this paper itself; it is a map of the determinants the underlying literature claims. The instrument class involved throughout is cryptocurrency spot (overwhelmingly Bitcoin; Ethereum, Litecoin, Dash, Monero, Ripple appear in individual studies). The cross-sectional factors the review catalogues — market, size, momentum, downside risk, MAX momentum, reversal, idiosyncratic volatility, liquidity (Table 7) — are drawn from long-short portfolio studies (e.g., Liu et al. 2020/2022 three-factor model; Zhang et al. 2021 downside risk), but the review reports neither implementation, nor whether long and short legs are separable, nor any execution timing. Whether any of these could be expressed in perpetuals rather than spot is not discussed; no transaction-cost or financing-cost figure appears in the paper.

### Strategies

The paper contains no strategies. In place of strategies, its units are the six factor categories; for each, the review reports the direction of the relationship and the studies asserting it, with no magnitudes.

**Supply and demand.** Bitcoin supply is asymptotically capped at 21 million (Polasik et al. 2015). Supply growth exerts downward pressure on price — negative relationship (Ciaian et al. 2016; Dubey 2022; Kristoufek 2015) — though Wang and Vergne (2017) find supply growth can drive price up, on the rationale that new cryptocurrencies appear more attractive. Demand-side drivers are claimed to have a stronger impact than supply (Ciaian et al. 2016). Demand proxies reported as positive for price/returns: growth of transactional need (Karaömer 2022), Bitcoin as payment method (Polasik et al. 2015), trading volume (Bouri et al. 2021), network factors — wallet users, payment accounts, transaction accounts (Liu & Tsyvinski 2021; Nakagawa & Sakemoto 2022) — and the emergence of other cryptocurrencies (Będowska-Sojka et al. 2021, Garman–Klass analysis) (Table 4).

**Technology.** Hash rate: positive for Bitcoin returns (Karaömer 2022); argued irrelevant by Kjaerland et al. (2018) because supply is deterministic by code, and no direct price effect in Fantazzini and Kolodin (2020). Mining difficulty: positive for Bitcoin price (Kristoufek 2015; Guizani & Nafti 2019; Li & Wang 2017), framed as a short-term price response with weak long-term impact. Halving: positive for price and, for the first halving, for price, market capitalisation and average transaction fees (Ramos & Zanko 2020; Meynkhard 2019; Ibrahim et al. 2020; Fantazzini & Kolodin 2020). Mining cost: positive (Sapkota & Grobys 2020, portfolio analysis; Chico-Frias 2021) versus no evidence (Baldan & Zen 2020; Liu & Tsyvinski 2021). Blockchain-information and protocol factors: small impact on Bitcoin price (Chen 2021), important for Ethereum (Kim et al. 2021), consensus-protocol technologies for Ripple (Chowdhury et al. 2022); Vo et al. (2022) claim pricing is less sensitive to technology in the long term (Table 5).

**Economics.** Exchange rates: negative with Bitcoin price (Karaömer 2022; Zhu et al. 2017; Polasik et al. 2015 for USD and EUR; Poyser 2019 for CNY); positive for returns under LASSO for JPY/USD, CNY/USD, USD/EUR, GBP/USD (Panagiotidis et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2022); insignificant at 95% confidence, GBP significant at 90% (Almansour et al. 2020). Interest rates: higher Federal Reserve rate negative for Bitcoin price (Havidz et al. 2021; Zhu et al. 2017; Jareño et al. 2020) but positive for returns under LASSO (Panagiotidis et al. 2018); US federal funds and Chinese interbank rates increased volatility of stablecoins and cryptocurrencies (Nguyen et al. 2022, GARCH/EGARCH/fixed-effect). CPI: long-term negative (Zhu et al. 2017), short-term positive as hedging asset (Wang et al. 2022), CPI news insignificant (Corbet et al. 2020, sentiment index). Gold and oil: gold positive (Jareño et al. 2020; Lamothe-Fernandez et al. 2020; Ciaian et al. 2016; Pogudin et al. 2019); oil reported both positive (Ciaian et al. 2016; Pogudin et al. 2019; Panagiotidis et al. 2018) and negative (Jareño et al. 2020; Ciaian et al. 2016, rationale: higher oil prices shrink budgets available for investment). Stock markets: Dow Jones positive (Ciaian et al. 2016; Lamothe-Fernandez et al. 2020) but long-term negative in Zhu et al. (2017); S&P 500 positive (Bakas et al. 2022; Jareño et al. 2020; Nguyen 2022; Vo et al. 2022); Chinese stock index positive (Bouoiyour & Selmi 2015); Nikkei positive (Panagiotidis et al. 2018); equity-market fear positive for Bitcoin, Ethereum, Litecoin (Anamika et al. 2021); negative but insignificant (Havidz et al. 2021). Economic policy uncertainty: negative for Bitcoin price/returns (Hasan et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2022 Cryptocurrency Policy Uncertainty Index; Kalyvas et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022; Jareño et al. 2020), with European EPU the most important variable for Bitcoin returns in Panagiotidis et al. (2018); CBDC-related policy positive (Scharnowski 2022) (Table 6).

**Market volatility.** Systematic risk: positive cross-sectional relation between downside risk and future returns (Zhang et al. 2021, univariate portfolio analysis); returns driven by three common risk factors — cryptocurrency market returns, market capitalisation (size), and momentum (Liu et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022; Wang & Chong 2021 adds value/growth); MAX momentum (Li et al. 2021); reversal (Jia et al. 2022); idiosyncratic volatility (Leirvik 2022; Liu & Tsyvinski 2021); liquidity (Zhang & Li 2020). Unsystematic risk and events: hacking events negative (Corbet et al. 2019); terrorist attacks positive (Almaqableh et al. 2022); COVID-19 positive and significant for Bitcoin price in the short term (Çelik et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2022) and for conditional volatility (Apergis 2022; Nguyen 2022; Sarkodie et al. 2022); hacker attacks and COVID-19 highly correlated with returns (Demiralay & Golitsis 2021); exchange-platform risk, e.g. the Mt. Gox collapse, negative (Zhu et al. 2017) (Table 7).

**Investors' attributes.** Attention: positive for price (Smales 2021; Al Guindy 2021) and predicts future volatility via VAR (Zhu et al. 2021; Mohamed 2021); attractiveness indicators positive with time variation (Guizani & Nafti 2019; Hakim das Neves 2020); popularity positive in the short term (Karaömer 2022; Garcia et al. 2014; Nepp & Karpeko 2022). Sentiment: positive opinion/sentiment positively correlated with pricing (Kjaerland et al. 2018; Patel et al. 2020; Gurrib & Kamalov 2022; Sapkota 2022); positive word of mouth contributes to price bubbles (Garcia et al. 2014); positive feedback trading increases volatility (Wang et al. 2022); negative sentiment significant for returns and volume (Huynh 2021); "buzz" negatively associated with returns (Wang & Vergne 2017); Elon Musk tweets influential (Shahzad et al. 2022); crypto-expert predictions significant (Gerritsen et al. 2022); discussion of unethical practices negative (Barth et al. 2020); developers' emotions drive volatility in Bitcoin and Ethereum (Bartolucci et al. 2020); emotional factors predict trading volume and return volatility (Ahn & Kim 2021); investors' mood linked to volatility (Rubbaniy et al. 2022) (Table 8).

**Social media.** Wikipedia views positive and statistically significant for Bitcoin price (Figà-Talamanca & Patacca 2020; Ciaian et al. 2016 "strong impact"; Phillips & Gorse 2018, long-term positive). Google Trends/Search: positive for returns (Polasik et al. 2015; Aslanidis et al. 2022; Panagiotidis et al. 2018 — Google Search the most important variable for Bitcoin returns; Chuffart 2022; Bakas et al. 2022; Ciaian et al. 2016), with one contrary finding that Bitcoin and Ethereum price movements were negatively affected by Google Trends search volume (Smuts 2019) (Table 9).

### Test setup

There is no test setup in the econometric sense. The review's procedure is the PRISMA approach: search terms ("cryptocurrency" OR "encryption currency" OR "digital money" OR "digital currency") AND ("factor" OR "determine") AND "(price)" across Scopus, Web of Science and EBSCOhost; only peer-reviewed, English-language journal articles, published before August 2022 (Method). Screening: 563 initial hits (Scopus 313, Web of Science 72, EBSCOhost 178); 185 duplicates discarded in EndNote X9; 213 removed on title/abstract; 165 assessed for eligibility, of which 76 excluded for not examining pricing factors, 18 removed as conference papers, 15 excluded as not English, leaving 56; 32 more added from reference lists, for 88 total (Method, Figure 1). No data, universe, survivorship, delisting, cost, or benchmark considerations are described, because none are used.

### Results

The review's "results" are its counts and its factor map, not performance figures. Publishing trends: 88 articles in 54 journals; Finance Research Letters leads with 27, then PLoS One (4), and 2 each in Financial Innovation, Journal of Risk and Financial Management, Journal of Behavioural Finance, Studies in Economics and Finance, International Review of Financial Analysis, with the remaining 47 across other journals (Table 1). Geography: 18 regions, Europe 29, UK 12, China 12, USA 9, UAE 4, Russia 3, India 3, Canada 3, Australia 3, South Korea 2 (Table 2). Methods: 48 distinct methods across the corpus; VAR most used (9), then ARDL (6), GARCH (5), three-factor model (4), fixed-effect model (3), wavelet coherence (3); OLS regression, vector error correction, asset pricing model, cost of production model and text analytic approach used twice each (Table 3; Discussion).

What the authors conclude from this map: investors' sentiment is a key factor; cryptocurrency can complement the existing financial industry, and Bitcoin has safe-haven-like properties (gold connection in Jareño et al. 2020; greater-fool and momentum theories in Kjaerland et al. 2018); practitioners can use the consolidated determinants to construct price-prediction models and trace price movements to avoid large-change events (Implications). The paper does not favour any single factor as dominant beyond noting that demand-side drivers are stronger than supply (Ciaian et al. 2016) and that sentiment is key (Discussion).

### Robustness

The paper has no robustness section; it is a review and performs no sensitivity analysis of its own.

### Specification search

The review is itself a search over the literature rather than over specifications. Reported counts: 88 articles (56 screened-in plus 32 from reference lists); 48 distinct research methods; 54 journals; 18 regions; six factor categories. It does not state how many candidate articles were examined at each step beyond the screening counts above (563 → 185 duplicates → 213 title/abstract exclusions → 165 eligibility assessments → 76 + 18 + 15 exclusions). Table 1 (journal × year) and the country × year matrix (Table 2) are only partially legible in the extracted text; the review does not report results for all cells it tabulates.

### Limitations and future work

The authors' own limitations: only peer-reviewed articles were considered (conference papers, government reports, theses excluded); only three databases were searched; non-English studies excluded; some relevant articles may have been missed given the "arbitrary nature" of the keyword, title and abstract inclusion/exclusion criteria (Limitations of the study and future research). Their future-research directions: examine other coins beyond Bitcoin (e.g., Dogecoin, Litecoin); technological dimensions of cryptocurrency; links between cryptocurrency and the existing financial market and economic policy; quantifying sentiment factors; and neglected factors such as cultural and political drivers and financial-technology development (Discussion).

### Extraction gaps

Table 1 (journal × date distribution) and parts of Tables 3 and 8 are garbled or truncated in the extracted text (rows cut mid-line, "N/A" placeholders where a value likely existed, e.g., Kjaerland et al. 2018 hash rate; Nguyen et al. 2022 federal rate). Author names are corrupted by character encoding throughout ("KaraOMer €" for Karaömer, "B e ˛ dowska-S ojka", "ÇElik", "Jare no ~"); these are presentation artifacts. The Method states the search covered studies "published before August 2022" while the Discussion says "before September 2022"; the paper contains no automated instructions or text addressed to an AI system, and nothing in the document asks the reader to disregard extraction rules.
