**Category: comparison** — fourteen prediction models (12 machine learning, 2 linear), estimated on a common panel of 25 factors, evaluated on out-of-sample statistical accuracy and then converted into decile-sorted long-short and long-only portfolios whose performance is compared. No publication date appears anywhere in the extracted text.

## Claim

The paper predicts weekly cross-sectional cryptocurrency returns with a panel of 8 macroeconomic and 17 characteristic factors using 14 models (OLS, OLS-3, PLS, LASSO, Ridge, ENet, RF, GBRT, KNN, NN1–NN5). Out-of-sample predictability is unusually high relative to equities: full-sample $R^2_{oos}$ of 25.265% for random forest and 18.704% for the best neural net (NN4), versus 0.33% (RF) and 0.40% (NN3) reported by Gu et al. (2020) for the U.S. stock market (§3.2, Table 3). Predictability rises with market cap: RF reaches $R^2_{oos}$ = 42.762% for mega-cap cryptocurrencies (Table 3). SHAP analysis identifies the change rates of the market-value-to-realized-value ratio (MRR), new addresses (NA), and active addresses (AA) as the most influential predictors, all positively associated with expected returns (§3.3–3.4, Figures 2–4). Decile portfolios formed on predicted returns beat linear-model portfolios; neural-network portfolios (NN5 long-short, NN4 long-only) post the largest mean weekly returns, PLS the largest long-short Sharpe ratio (Table 5). Sample: weekly data, January 1, 2014 to March 31, 2023; training 2014–2017, validation 2018–2020, testing 2021–2023 (§2, Table 5 note).

## Fit with our constraints

Instrument class is a cross-section of cryptocurrencies drawn from Coinmarketcap.com; the prediction targets are weekly returns on the coins themselves. Both long-short and long-only portfolios are constructed and reported separately (§4); the paper explicitly notes that shorting is not mechanically available ("implementing long-short portfolios is difficult in the current cryptocurrency market due to the absence of a short-selling mechanism", §4), so the long-short results are an evaluation device and the long-only top-decile results are the implementable form. Value-weighted and equal-weighted portfolios are both reported, equal-weighted in Internet Appendix E (inferior, per §4). No leverage is used; long-short portfolios are zero-net-investment, long-only are fully invested. No transaction costs, borrow costs, or financing costs are modelled anywhere; all figures are gross. Replication would need: daily OHLC/volume/market-cap from Coinmarketcap (survivorship addressed because the site lists defunct coins, §2.1); paid on-chain data from IntoTheBlock (via App.intotheblock.com); WIND for equity/rates/commodity series; Google Trends for attention. Universe screens: coins must have complete price, market-cap and volume data and market cap ≥ $10,000,000; no point-in-time reconstruction of membership is described.

## Strategies

All fourteen models share one setup (§2.2). The prediction model is $r_{i,t+1} = \mathbb{E}_t[r_{i,t+1}] + \epsilon_{i,t+1}$ with $\mathbb{E}[r_{i,t+1}] = g(z_{i,t})$, $z_{i,t}$ a $P$-dimensional predictor vector, $i = 1,\dots,N$, $t = 1,\dots,T$; $g(\cdot)$ unspecified. Inputs are Z-score standardized across the training, validation, and testing samples. Hyperparameters are tuned on the validation sample (2018–2020); the testing sample (2021–2023) never enters estimation or tuning. Prediction accuracy is scored by $R^2_{oos} = 1 - \sum_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{T}_3}(r_{i,t}-\hat{r}_{i,t})^2 / \sum_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{T}_3} r_{i,t}^2$ (Eq. 3), and pairwise accuracy differences by Diebold–Mariano tests (§3.1, A.10). Portfolios: at each week's end, predict next-week returns, sort into deciles, take decile 10 (buy) and decile 1 (sell) for long-short, decile 10 only for long-only; weekly rebalance; turnover is not reported (§4).

### Linear benchmarks — OLS and OLS-3
OLS fits $g(z_{i,t};\theta) = z'_{i,t}\theta$ by least squares (l2 loss, closed form; A.1). OLS-3 is OLS restricted to the market, size, and momentum factors RMRF, SMB, UMD. Rationale is benchmark rather than economic: these establish the linear floor and, per the authors, isolate the incremental value of the full factor set. Full-sample $R^2_{oos}$: 3.901% (OLS) vs 0.044% (OLS-3), which the authors read as the three Fama–French-style factors capturing little of the linear predictability (Table 3).

### Regularized/component models — PLS, LASSO, Ridge, ENet
PLS compresses the $P$ predictors into $K \ll P$ components by solving $w_j = \max_w \text{Cov}^2(R, Zw)$ s.t. $w'w = 1$, $\text{Cov}(Zw, Zw_l) = 0$ for $l < j$, with $K$ tuned on validation (A.2). LASSO adds the l1 penalty $\lambda\sum|\theta_i|$ ($\rho = 0$, $\alpha = 1$); Ridge adds $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\sum\theta_i^2$ ($\rho = 1$, $\alpha = 0.8$); ENet mixes both ($\alpha = 0.5$, l1 ratio 0.5); $\lambda$ tuned on validation (A.3–A.5). PLS and Ridge reproduce OLS exactly on every sample (e.g., 3.901% full sample), which the authors attribute to all factors mattering, so shrinkage/rotation removes nothing; LASSO and ENet select nothing, with $R^2_{oos} \approx -0.0000032733$ full sample (footnote 3, Table 3). Despite this, PLS is the best long-short portfolio by Sharpe (Table 5).

### Tree models — RF and GBRT
RF averages $B$ regression trees over bootstrap samples, splitting each node on a random subset of features, $\hat{g}_b(z_{i,t};\hat{\theta}_b, L) = \sum_{k=1}^{2^L} \theta_b^{(k)} \mathbf{1}\{z_{i,t} \in C_k(L)\}$, with depth $L = 1{\sim}3$, trees $B = 100$, features $n = 50$ as stated (A.6; the feature count as printed conflicts with the 25 predictors, likely an extraction artifact). GBRT additively fits shallow trees to residuals with shrinkage: learning rate $\eta = 0.01$, depth $L = 1{\sim}3$, trees $B = 100$, features $n = 50$ (A.7). These are the best predictors: RF tops every sample (25.265% full, 42.762% mega, 27.372% large, 18.277% mid-cap); GBRT second (24.911%, 41.146%, 21.476%, 13.624%) (Table 3). DM tests show both significantly beat all non-tree models in the full sample (Table 4, partially legible).

### Neural networks — NN1–NN5
Fully connected feedforward nets with $n$ hidden layers: NN1 (32), NN2 (32,16), NN3 (32,16,8), NN4 (32,16,8,4), NN5 (32,16,8,4,2) neurons, sizes per the geometric pyramid rule (Masters 1993); sigmoid activation (best of five tried: Sigmoid, Tanh, ReLU, Leaky ReLU, ELU); AMSGrad with $\alpha = 0.001$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$; batch size 16; early stopping if validation error does not fall for 20 epochs, hard stop at 1000 epochs; initial weights small positive constants (A.9). $R^2_{oos}$ clusters at 15–19% full sample; depth does not monotonically help (NN4 best at 18.704% full sample and 36.090% mega-cap; NN5 best large-cap at 19.265%; NN2 best mid-cap at 15.326%) (Table 3).

### KNN
$k$-nearest-neighbor regression with $k = 10$, distance metric not specified (A.8). Mid-tier: 15.831% full sample, 21.762% mega-cap, 17.467% large, 13.726% mid-cap (Table 3).

### What the comparison establishes
Statistical accuracy ranks tree models first, neural nets second, KNN third, and linear/regularized last, with the ordering consistent across cap groups; every model's accuracy rises with market-cap tier. Economic (portfolio) value is ordered differently: PLS and the neural nets are the best portfolio models, with NN5 (long-short) and NN4 (long-only) highest mean returns and PLS the highest Sharpe (§4, Table 5). The authors conclude both tree and NN methods are usable in this market and note ML portfolios rose during the November 2021–June 2022 crash rather than falling (§4).

## Test setup

Weekly panel, Jan 1, 2014 – Mar 31, 2023, split chronologically into training (2014–2017), validation (2018–2020), testing (2021–2023); the 2021–2023 window is the only out-of-sample period and no post-publication sample exists. Universe: all Coinmarketcap-listed cryptocurrencies meeting data-completeness and ≥$10M market cap requirements; the market-return formula sums over 3,940 coins (B.7). Data sources: Coinmarketcap.com (prices, cap, volume; defunct coins included), App.intotheblock.com (on-chain fundamentals), WIND (SPX, VIX, USDX, GLD, WTI, EPU, TEU), Google Trends (attention; daily converted to weekly). Survivorship: addressed only by Coinmarketcap listing defunct coins; delisting handling otherwise not described. Costs: none; all results gross. Benchmark for the market factor: risk-free rate $RF_t$ subtracted from value-weighted market return (B.7). Cross-market comparison uses published $R^2_{oos}$ from Gu et al. (2020) and Leippold et al. (2022) rather than re-estimation (§3.2).

The authors conclude that ML portfolios dominate linear ones on every metric; that PLS and NNs are the best portfolio models while trees dominate statistically; and that cumulative-return tiers put NN5/GBRT/RF (long-short) and NN4/GBRT/RF (long-only) clearly ahead, with gaps widening after the third quarter of 2021 (§4).

## Robustness

Subgroup analysis by market-cap tier (mega >$1B, large $100M–$1B, mid $10M–$100M) is reported for all models (Table 3, §3.1.2–3.1.4) with DM tests per tier (Tables D.1–D.3) consistent with the $R^2_{oos}$ ranking. Equal-weighted portfolios are reported in Appendix E and are inferior to value-weighted (§4). Activation-function choice (five functions, Sigmoid best) and NN depth (five architectures) are compared (A.9). Cross-market comparison against published U.S. and Chinese stock-market results is made (§3.2). No subperiod robustness within the test window, no delayed-execution tests, no transaction-cost sensitivity, no alternative factor sets, and no parameter grids beyond the stated validation tuning are reported.

## Specification search

Fourteen models × four samples (full, mega, large, mid) are reported in full in Table 3 and Appendix D; fourteen models × two portfolio types (long-short/long-only) × two weightings (value-/equal-weighted) in Tables 5 and E.1 — all models' results are shown, not a selected subset. Five NN depths, five activation functions, and five Google-Trends keywords (with a 10-term relevance table, C.4.1) are examined. Hyperparameters were tuned on the validation sample (e.g., PLS component count $K$; penalty $\lambda$ for LASSO/Ridge/ENet), but the resulting tuned values are not reported.

## Limitations and future work

The paper contains no explicit limitations section. The one acknowledged practical constraint is the absence of a short-selling mechanism for implementing long-short portfolios in crypto markets (§4). No transaction costs are discussed and no capacity or turnover analysis is offered; the conclusion calls for further work only in the generic sense of providing guidance to investors, traders, and analysts (§5).

## Separable variants for our pipeline

For a comparison paper this list is the strategies again, which the paper supports: the 14 individual models (OLS, OLS-3, PLS, LASSO, Ridge, ENet, RF, GBRT, KNN, NN1–NN5) each define an independent predictive signal, with exact parameterizations in §A of the Internet Appendix. The three factors the paper finds most influential — change rates of MRR, NA, and AA (§3.3, Figures 2–3; definitions B.2 and B.1) — are individually testable conditioning variables with a documented positive sign on expected returns (§3.4). The full characteristic factor set (17 factors, definitions B.1–B.7) and macro set (8 factors, definitions C.1–C.4) are separable one-by-one. The GTCX index (5 weighted Google-Trends keywords, C.4) is a standalone attention signal. Portfolio constructions — decile long-short vs top-decile long-only, value- vs equal-weighted (§4, E) — are separable implementation choices.
