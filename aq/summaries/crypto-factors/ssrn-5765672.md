# Predicting cryptocurrency returns with machine learning (SSRN 5765672)

**Category:** comparison — 14 return-forecasting models (12 machine learning, 2 linear) evaluated against each other on a shared factor set, plus long-short and long-only decile portfolios built from each model's predicted returns. Working paper; "Preprint not peer reviewed" appears on every page.

## Claim

The paper estimates weekly cross-sectional cryptocurrency return forecasts $r_{i,t+1} = \mathbb{E}_t[r_{i,t+1}] + \epsilon_{i,t+1}$ with $\mathbb{E}[r_{i,t+1}] = g(z_{i,t})$ (§2.2, eq. 2), where $z_{i,t}$ is a 25-predictor vector of 8 macroeconomic and 17 characteristic factors, and $g(\cdot)$ is estimated by 14 models: OLS, OLS-3 (OLS on market, size, momentum only), PLS, LASSO, Ridge, ENet, random forest (RF), gradient boosted regression trees (GBRT), K-nearest neighbour (KNN), and neural networks with 1–5 layers (NN1–NN5). Out-of-sample R² on the 2021–2023 holdout is 25.265% for RF versus 3.901% for OLS (Table 3); tree models dominate, with predictability far above the U.S. and Chinese stock-market benchmarks taken from Gu et al. (2020) and Leippold et al. (2022) (§3.2). SHAP analysis identifies the change rates of the market-to-realized-value ratio (MRR), new addresses (NA), and active addresses (AA) as the three most influential predictors, all positively associated with expected returns (§3.3–3.4). Decile portfolios: RF/GBRT and NN4/NN5 have the highest cumulative returns; PLS has the highest long-short Sharpe; NN4 the highest long-only mean (Table 5, §4). No t-statistics or standard errors are reported for any R² or portfolio figure; the only significance testing is the pairwise Diebold–Mariano (1995) test on forecast accuracy (Table 4).

## Fit with our constraints

Instruments are spot cryptocurrencies only (all coins on Coinmarketcap.com; prices are volume-weighted exchange averages). The long-short portfolios require a short leg, which the paper itself flags as unrealistic: "implementing long-short portfolios is difficult in the current cryptocurrency market due to the absence of a short-selling mechanism" (§4) — so crypto futures/perpetuals would be the only way to express the short side; the paper does not discuss them. Long and short legs are not reported separately in any table: only the decile-10-minus-decile-1 spread is tabulated, and no decile-level return table exists. Long-only (top-decile) results are however reported separately for every model (Table 5). No leverage is used; long-short is described as zero-net-investment. Replication would need: Coinmarketcap daily price/market-cap/volume for roughly 3,940 distinct cryptocurrencies (the $\sum_{i=1}^{3940}$ in the market-return formula, §B.7) from 2014–2023 (some defunct, which mitigates survivorship bias); IntoTheBlock on-chain data (paid API); WIND financial-market data; Google Trends (five-keyword index); the Baker et al. (2016) EPU and Baker et al. (2021) TEU indices; and a risk-free rate (source not further specified). Returns are gross; no transaction-cost, fee, borrow, or funding model is described anywhere, and turnover is never quantified.

## Strategies

All 14 models share one protocol (§2.2, §A): data split chronologically into training (2014–2017), validation (2018–2020, used only for hyperparameter tuning), and testing (2021–2023, used only for evaluation); all inputs Z-score standardized across the three samples; the target is next-week return; predictors are the 25 weekly factor changes. At the end of each week the model predicts next-week returns; coins are sorted into deciles; value-weighted (and equal-weighted) portfolios are formed, held one week, rebalanced weekly; long-short buys decile 10 and sells decile 1, long-only buys decile 10 alone (§4, following Gu et al. 2020). The signal-to-trade gap is one week (prediction at end of week $t$ for week $t{+}1$); intra-week timing is not specified. The factor set itself (Table 1, Appendix B/C): characteristic — NA and TV (network; AA, ZBA, AAR, NT, TFE, ATF, TA, ATBT, CUTO, SUTO, HTTC are also defined in Appendix B.1), MRR and NTR (finance), LHN, LHI, LHO (ownership), NF and TF (exchange), GT (social), plus RMRF, SMB, UMD (market/size/momentum); macroeconomic — EPU, TEU, SPX, VIX, USDX, GLD, WTI, and GTCX. Most change rates are $\Delta X_{t+1} = (X_{t+1} - X_t)/X_t$ though NT is written with a one-period lag, and whether the "$t{+}1$"-dated change is used as predictor $z_{i,t}$ is ambiguous. The paper does not discuss who is on the other side of any trade, and no counterparty or market-making interpretation is offered.

### Linear benchmarks: OLS, OLS-3

OLS estimates $g(z;\theta) = z'\theta$ by least squares with an $l_2$ loss ($\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{NT}\sum_i\sum_t [r_{i,t+1} - g(z_{i,t};\theta)]^2$, §A.1). OLS-3 is the same regression restricted to RMRF, SMB, UMD. OLS achieves 3.901% R²_oos versus 0.044% for OLS-3 (Table 3), which the authors read as evidence that market, size, and momentum alone are insufficient.

### Regularized and component linear models: PLS, LASSO, Ridge, ENet

PLS builds $K$ predictive components maximizing $\text{Cov}^2(R, Zw)$ subject to unit norm and orthogonality (eqs. in §A.2), with $K$ tuned on validation. Ridge ($\phi(\theta;\lambda) = \frac{1}{2}\lambda\sum\theta_i^2$, $\alpha = 0.8$), LASSO ($\phi(\theta;\lambda) = \lambda\sum|\theta_i|$, $\alpha = 1$), and ENet ($\phi(\theta;\lambda,\rho) = \lambda(1-\rho)\sum|\theta_i| + \frac{1}{2}\lambda\rho\sum\theta_i^2$, $\alpha = 0.5$, $l_1$ ratio 0.5) are penalized linear models with $\lambda$ tuned on validation (§A.3–A.5). PLS and Ridge replicate OLS's 3.901% exactly; LASSO and ENet have effectively zero R²_oos (-0.0000032733) (Table 3).

### Tree ensembles: RF, GBRT

RF bagging: each tree averages within $2^L$ partitions, splitting on random subsamples of size $\sqrt{P}$ of features; hyperparameters depth $L = 1{\sim}3$, trees $B = 100$, features $n = 50$ (§A.6). GBRT adds shallow trees with depth 1–3, 100 trees, 50 features, learning rate 0.01 (§A.7). RF posts the best full-sample R²_oos of any model: 25.265% vs GBRT's 24.911% (Table 3), and the margin over GBRT widens in mega-caps (42.762% vs 41.146%).

### KNN

10-nearest-neighbour regression (neighbours = 10, §A.8). Full-sample R²_oos 15.831%, marginally below NN1's 15.837% (Table 3); it is the only method whose predictability falls below the full-sample figure when restricted to mid-caps (13.726% vs 13.726% — reported as "smaller than that of the full sample" with equal values as extracted, Table 3).

### Neural networks: NN1–NN5

Fully connected feedforward nets with 1 to 5 hidden layers sized by the geometric pyramid rule: NN1: 32; NN2: 32-16; NN3: 32-16-8; NN4: 32-16-8-4; NN5: 32-16-8-4-2 neurons (§A.9). AMSGrad with $\alpha = 0.001$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$; batch size 16; sigmoid activation (best of Sigmoid, Tanh, ReLU, Leaky ReLU, ELU); early stopping after 20 epochs without validation improvement, cap 1000 epochs. NN4 is the best net in the full sample (18.704%) and for mega-caps (36.090%); NN5 best in large-caps (19.265%); NN2 best in mid-caps (15.326%) (Table 3).

### What the comparison establishes

Across the full sample and every cap group, RF (and generally GBRT) dominate on R²_oos; neural networks are second; KNN third; linear and regularized models weakest. DM tests (Table 4, Appendix D) mark tree models as significantly more accurate than the linear/regularized families, while differences among neural networks and between RF and GBRT are largely insignificant. The authors conclude predictability in crypto is "substantially stronger than that documented in equity markets." Portfolio value diverges from forecast accuracy: PLS (not RF) has the highest long-short Sharpe, and NN4/NN5 have the highest cumulative returns — the paper therefore favours neural-network- and PLS-based portfolios for investment purposes rather than the statistically best forecasters (§4).

## Test setup

Sample: all cryptocurrencies on Coinmarketcap.com with complete price, market-cap, and volume data from January 1, 2014 to March 31, 2023, excluding market caps below $10,000,000 (§2.1); 3,940 distinct coins enter the market-return aggregation, but the per-week cross-section size is not stated. Weekly returns are computed from daily closes because the fundamental data is weekly (footnote 1). Coinmarketcap lists active and defunct coins, which the authors say "alleviate[s] concerns about survivorship bias"; no delisting-return handling is described, and universe membership is not documented as point-in-time reconstructed. Data sources: Coinmarketcap.com (prices/mcap/volume), App.intotheblock.com (on-chain fundamentals), WIND (financial-market data), Google Trends (search data), policyuncertainty.com (EPU, TEU). No transaction costs or financing costs anywhere; all portfolio figures are gross. There is no portfolio benchmark (no buy-and-hold crypto index comparison); statistical benchmarks are the Gu et al. (2020) U.S. and Leippold et al. (2022) Chinese stock-market numbers quoted in §3.2.

Cumulative-return trajectories (§4, Figures 6–7) split long-short portfolios into four tiers — NN5/GBRT/RF, then KNN/Ridge, then PLS/OLS/LASSO/ENet, then OLS-3 — and long-only into five, with NN4/GBRT/RF on top; all machine-learning portfolios, "especially those based on neural networks and trees," are reported to have risen in cumulative terms during the November 2021–June 2022 market crash rather than falling.

## Robustness

There is no section labelled robustness; the robustness-like evidence is (i) market-cap subgroups (mega/large/mid) covering all models (Table 3, Appendix D) — predictability is highest for mega-caps and weakest for mid-caps, for every model; (ii) the cross-market comparison against published U.S. and Chinese stock-market figures (§3.2); (iii) equal-weighted portfolio replication (Appendix E); and (iv) DM tests for each subgroup (Appendix D). Degradation is reported honestly: LASSO/ENet have zero predictability; mid-cap results fall below full-sample for most models; KNN loses its edge in mid-caps; the neural-network "depth benefit" fails beyond NN4 in most samples. No delayed-execution test, alternative factor sets, alternative split points, or post-publication period are examined.

## Specification search

Two linear + 12 ML model families; 5 neural-network depths; 5 activation functions compared (sigmoid selected, §A.9); RF/GBRT depth grids 1–3 with 100 trees; validation-set tuning for all penalty, component-count, and network hyperparameters. Results are reported for every model in every condition examined — full, mega-, large-, and mid-cap R²_oos (Table 3), portfolio statistics for both weightings (Tables 5, E.1), and full DM matrices (Tables 4, D.1–D.3) — rather than selected subsets. No count is given of total fits run.

## Limitations and future work

The paper has no standalone limitations section; the conclusion frames the study as providing "guidance for the pricing of cryptocurrency assets." The one constraint acknowledged in the body is that long-short portfolios cannot be implemented given the absence of a shorting mechanism in the crypto market, motivating the long-only variants (§4). Nothing is said by the authors about costs, capacity, data latency, or out-of-sample decay beyond the fixed holdout.

## Separable variants for our pipeline

This is a comparison paper, so the separable list is largely the model signals again, plus the factor inventory:

- Daily/weekly predicted-return signals from RF, GBRT, NN4, NN5, and PLS (the five models the authors favour), each as a decile-sort signal (Table 3, Table 5).
- Long-only top-decile versions of the same signals (Table 5, LO columns) — tradable in spot without a short leg.
- Single-factor signals implied by SHAP importance: MRR (market value / average purchase cost of each address, §B.2), NA and AA change rates (§B.1), all positively signed; NN4 additionally highlights AA, and tree models highlight NA (§3.3–3.4).
- GTCX, the five-keyword Google-Trends cryptocurrency index ($GTCX_t = \sum_{i=1}^5 \frac{GT_{i,t}}{TGT_t} \times GT_{i,t}$, §C.4), as an investor-attention signal.
- The Fama–French-style crypto SMB/UMD constructions (§B.7) as candidate factor definitions, though inside this paper they are inputs, not strategies.

- The document contained no instructions addressed to an AI system.
- Figure 1 (factor correlation heatmap) is image-only. The surrounding text states one correlation "is exceptionally high at 0.91," then gives AAR–NT = 0.74 and SPX–VIX = -0.39; the pair with 0.91 is not identifiable from the text.
- Table 2's Min column is garbled (values missing or merged); several macro-factor statistics are unreadable.
- Diebold–Mariano matrices (Tables 4, D.1–D.3) are badly scrambled by extraction: row/column labels and cell placement cannot be reliably matched, so DM values are cited only at the level of significance patterns.
- Section B.5 of the appendix is absent (text jumps from B.4 Exchange to B.6 Social).
- Sharpe units are printed as "annualized Sharpe ratio (%)" with values like 63.698; whether this means 0.637 or 63.7× annualized is ambiguous as extracted.
- Characteristic-factor naming is inconsistent: Table 1 lists "active addresses volume (TV)" while AA, ZBA, AAR, NT, and TV appear separately in Table 2 and the appendix definitions; the exact membership of the "17 characteristic factors" is implied (Table 2's 14 stat rows plus RMRF, SMB, UMD) but never stated explicitly.
- Equation (2) and several appendix formulas contain unrecovered glyphs (e.g. the cryptocurrency index $i$ and layer notation in §A.9 rendered as boxes).
- The timing convention of the factor change rates (t vs t+1) is ambiguous; NT's change rate uses a different lag convention from the other factors.
