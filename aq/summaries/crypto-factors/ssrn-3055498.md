Category: **comparison** — three newly proposed cryptocurrency factors (momentum, value, carry) are defined, tested individually, and then blended, under two weighting schemes (equal-weighted, risk-weighted), two information sets (cross-sectional z-scores vs. "complete" longitudinal z-scores), and finally combined with the paper's own benchmarks. The benchmarks are also the paper's construction, so every "alpha" is relative to a self-built benchmark, not a published index.

## Claim

The paper applies factor investing to cryptocurrencies for the first time ("to our knowledge"; §V), defining momentum as prior-week return, value as market value relative to on-chain transaction volume, and carry as protocol-driven coin issuance (§II.D). On 11 coins with daily data from April 28, 2013 to September 2017 (data availability per September 2017, Table 1; BTC and LTC have the longest histories at 1,589 daily observations each), rebalanced weekly, momentum is "by far the most powerful individual factor" (Abstract), and blended multi-factor portfolios perform better still: three of the four blends (both risk-weighted versions and the complete equal-weighted version) show alphas significant against all three benchmarks (Table 5). The strongest single variant is risk-weighted, complete momentum: annualized average return 19.8%, Sharpe 1.70, alpha vs. the capitalization-weighted benchmark of 21.33% (p=0.001) (Table 4). All portfolios are 10% invested in crypto and hold 90% cash at zero cash return (§II.B); no leverage is used. The headline alphas are annualized intercepts from regressions of portfolio returns on benchmark returns, with Newey-West HAC p-values (Tables 4–5). Sample end (September 2017) is about one month before the paper's date (October 28, 2017); nearly nine years have elapsed since.

## Fit with our constraints

Instrument class is crypto spot only; the paper states that "no derivatives (such as futures) are available to 'leverage' these assets" (§I) and does not discuss perpetuals. The factor portfolios "go short" via negative weights, and the paper acknowledges "cryptocurrencies cannot yet be shorted at scale" (§IV); whether the short leg could be expressed in futures or perpetuals is not addressed. Long and short legs are never reported separately — only net portfolio returns and attribution alphas — and the "combined" portfolios resolve the shorting problem by setting any negative currency weight to zero rather than by shorting (§IV). Leverage: none; gross exposure is fixed at 10% by construction, chosen from a CRRA calibration (risk aversion 2–3, assumed 0.3 Sharpe, footnote 5) with returns rescaled only for that calibration, not for the reported results. Replication needs: coinmetrics.io raw data (prices, market values, on-chain transaction volumes, coin issuance rates) and cryptocompare.com (maximum coin supply), Table 1/Appendix 2. Universe membership is not point-in-time: the 11 coins are exactly those with data on coinmetrics.io per September 2017 (Appendix 2), i.e., selected with full hindsight, and survivorship/delisting is not addressed. No microcap, penny-stock, or liquidity screens are applied beyond data availability.

## Strategies

Common construction (§II.B, §IV): weekly rebalancing; because there are 7 possible definitions of a "week," each portfolio is implemented 7 times with a different rebalancing weekday, and all results pool the 7 return series. Portfolios start once at least four currencies are available and the universe grows as coins appear. Holding period is 7 trading days. The gap between signal date and trade date is not specified beyond "information available at the time of rebalancing" (§IV); factors use trailing data through the rebalancing date. EW portfolios give absolute weight 10%/n, signed by the standardized factor; RW portfolios divide the factor by the longest possible trailing volatility estimate for the currency (realized vol of daily overlapping 7-day returns, "telescoping" window, 30-day burn-in, footnote 6) and rescale so the sum of absolute weights is 10% (§IV).

### Momentum

Signal: "our raw momentum factor is the prior week's return for each currency" (§II.D.1). Standardized by longitudinal z-score per currency (complete portfolios) or by cross-sectional z-score at each date (cross-sectional portfolios) (§II.E, §IV). EW complete portfolios are net long the market when the standardized factor is positive, net short when negative; EW cross-sectional portfolios are long the top half and short the bottom half of the cross-sectional rank, market-neutral by construction (§IV). Rationale: continuation, framed by reference to Asness et al. (2013) and to Rohrbach et al. (2017), who found cross-sectional momentum works in cryptocurrencies; the paper does not commit to a behavioural or risk-based story, and identifies no counterparty. Results: complete momentum is the best individual factor — EW complete averages 6.3% with Sharpe 0.77 and alphas 9.88% (p=0.001) vs. EW, 9.72% (p=0.005) vs. CW, 10.26% (p=0.001) vs. RW; RW complete averages 19.8% with Sharpe 1.70 and alphas 16.88%/21.33%/16.91% (all p=0.001) vs. EW/CW/RW (Table 4). Cross-sectional momentum alphas are positive but mostly insignificant, except vs. the CW benchmark (5.21%, p=0.046; 9.03%, p=0.045). Annualized notional trading volume: 80–90% (Appendix 5).

### Value

Signal: raw metric MTV ("Market to Transaction Value") = ratio of current market value to the trailing 7-day average of $-valued on-chain transactions on the currency's blockchain (§II.D.2); a high numeric value means the currency is expensive. The metric is z-scored longitudinally per currency, since raw levels are not comparable across currencies (on-chain data include change-address transactions, so raw volumes are an upper bound; §II.A). No value data for XMR. Rationale: mean reversion of valuation toward a fair equilibrium, in the spirit of price-to-earnings for equities; the paper argues MTV mean-reverts at much higher frequency than equity valuation cycles (§II.D.2). Results: "as a single factor, value disappoints — the alphas are positive throughout but far from statistically significant" (§IV): best case is EW complete, 8.0% average, Sharpe 1.13, alpha vs. CW 4.88% (p=0.148) (Table 4). Turnover 30–50% annualized (Appendix 5).

### Carry

Signal: "raw carry as the *negative* of the sum total coin issuance over the preceding 7 days, divided by the coins outstanding at the beginning of that 7 day period" (§II.D.3); high carry means low mining-based inflation. Because carry is definitionally close to an expected return, it is standardized by the currency's return volatility rather than by its own time series; complete portfolios then apply a "panel z score" using the mean and volatility of pooled carry across all currencies up to that point, which preserves the sign and the cross-currency structure (footnote 15). No carry data for XEM. Rationale: if demand is unchanged, a currency loses nominal value at its current inflation run rate; the paper notes this is not the conventional futures-based carry (no futures exist for these assets; §II.D.3, Appendix 3). Results: EW complete carry averages 16.4% with Sharpe 2.19 and alpha vs. CW of 10.06% (p=0.022); RW complete averages 8.9%, Sharpe 1.48, alphas significant only at 10% (0.087–0.103). Cross-sectional EW carry is the worst portfolio in the paper: average −5.5%, Sharpe −0.90 (Table 4). Turnover 10–25% annualized (Appendix 5).

### What the comparison establishes

Complete (longitudinal) portfolios are "universally better" than their cross-sectional counterparts in return, Sharpe, and alpha significance — the longitudinal dimension makes the contribution (§IV). Momentum dominates individually. The 1/3 blends (EW blends are 1/3 to each factor portfolio; RW blends weight the three factor portfolios inversely to their trailing realized volatility, rescaled to sum to one) beat momentum alone, which the authors read as indirect confirmation that carry and value add value despite weak standalone performance (§IV). Risk-weighting is not consistently better than equal-weighting in Sharpe terms, contrary to the authors' hypothesis (§IV). The combined portfolios (factor portfolio added to the same-weight benchmark, negative weights truncated to zero) dominate the benchmarks: Sharpe up to 2.91 vs. 2.56 for the best benchmark, BM-EW (Tables 2, 5).

## Test setup

Sample: April 28, 2013 – September 2017 (per-coin first-observation dates in Table 1; availability per September 2017, Appendix 2). No out-of-sample or post-publication period is reported; the Section IV portfolios are "out of sample" only in the sense of using recursively available information (standardization from data up to the rebalancing date, §II.E), on the same sample. Instruments: 11 cryptocurrencies (BTC, DASH, DCR, DOGE, ETC, ETH, LTC, PIVX, XEM, XMR, ZEC); XEM (no carry) and XMR (no value) are excluded from the regressions (§III). Data: coinmetrics.io for prices and on-chain data; cryptocompare.com for maximum coin supply (Table 1). Returns are computed from daily overlapping 7-day holding periods (§II.B); Table 1 weekly statistics are not annualized. Benchmarks: BM-EW (equal weights, 25.6% average, Sharpe 2.56), BM-CW (market-value weights, 9.9%, 1.46), BM-RW (inverse trailing-volatility weights, 21.6%, 2.45), all 10% invested (Table 2). Attribution regressions are run against all three benchmarks; alphas and IRs annualized, p-values Newey-West HAC. Headline results are gross; transaction costs are only estimated in Appendix 5; financing cost is zero by the 90%-cash convention; survivorship is not addressed.

In the full-sample regressions (Table 3), the weekly model is the "sweet spot": BASE_7 gives momentum 0.119 (p=0.01), carry 0.050 (p<0.01), value 0.040 (p=0.083), adjusted R² 0.019, all coefficients signed as hypothesized; one standard deviation of trailing momentum predicts forward returns about 0.12 standard deviations above average (§III). Long-horizon (90-day) momentum and value load negatively in most models (e.g., BASE_90 momentum −0.157, p=0.006, adjusted R² 0.128), which the authors attribute to 90 days being "quite long term" in the high-volatility universe; carry is the weakest factor in-sample (§III). Commonly reported metrics absent throughout: no t-statistics for Sharpe ratios, no net-of-cost headline numbers, no capacity estimates, no long- vs. short-leg decomposition. The authors' conclusion is that factor investing "can earn excess returns in the cryptocurrency market" (Abstract) and that the combined portfolios dominate the benchmarks on absolute and risk-adjusted return while "participating in the overall market" (§IV); they caution strongly against extrapolating benchmark performance (Table 2 discussion).

## Robustness

Appendix 4 reruns the Table 3 regressions (7-day only) and the Tables 4–5 backtests under: (i) non-overlapping periods under all 7 rebalancing-day definitions, reporting the median; (ii) 2.5% winsorization (after standardization; for backtests, winsorizing portfolio returns); (iii) dropping each of the 11 coins in turn (11 versions, median reported); (iv) a 90-day instead of 30-day burn-in; and (v) ranked-data regressions. Results: winsorizing "meaningfully" reduces carry significance (p=0.35) and weakens value (p=0.16); on ranked data the value effect becomes negligible and insignificant; isolated single-factor portfolios lose significance, most often under the 90-day burn-in; "we observed no deterioration in the performance of the combined multi-factor portfolios" (Appendix 4). Panel-data alternatives (currency fixed/random effects via F-tests and Breusch-Pagan) give essentially identical factor estimates to pooled OLS, except BASE_30 with currency fixed effects, where momentum turns positive/significant and value negative/significant (Appendix 4).

## Specification search

The paper reports every model it runs: 8 regressions (4 holding periods × BASE/FIT selection, Table 3); 12 individual factor portfolios (3 factors × 2 weightings × 2 information sets, Table 4); 4 composite and 4 combined portfolios (Table 5); 7 rebalancing-day versions of each portfolio, pooled. The FIT variable-selection procedure (footnote 13) is forward-stepwise on absolute correlation, re-estimating after each step and stopping when not all selected regressors are significant at 5%. No results are withheld as "not shown." The authors note they "tried to define each factor, to the degree possible, according to the same weekly 'rhythm' as our return horizon... to avoid overfitting" (§II.D).

## Limitations and future work

The authors list three avenues (§V): risk management — they were "unenthused by the value-add from risk management applications" and expect fat-tailed returns to challenge conventional volatility forecasting; portfolio construction — exceptionally high-volatility assets may require broadening traditional multi-asset construction (they argue high volatility is a virtue, since small allocations suffice); and time — the sample is short, the universe is evolving rapidly, and they "surmise that our initial study will be outdated in a year or two."

## Separable variants for our pipeline

The comparison's three factors are each independently testable, with their exact definitions: momentum = prior 7-day return (§II.D.1); value = MTV, market value ÷ trailing 7-day average $-valued on-chain transaction volume (§II.D.2); carry = −(7-day coin issuance) ÷ coins outstanding at period start, volatility-standardized (§II.D.3). Each is separable along the paper's own design axes: EW vs. RW weighting, cross-sectional vs. complete (longitudinal) standardization, standalone vs. 1/3-blend vs. benchmark-combined with shorts truncated (§IV). The in-sample regressions additionally define factor variants at 1-, 30-, and 90-day horizons (Table 3) — the paper reports that short-horizon momentum/valuation work but are offset by negatively signed long-horizon terms, and that the 90-day momentum coefficient is negative and significant (BASE_90: −0.157, p=0.006).
