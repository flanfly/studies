Category: **comparison** — three factors (momentum, value, carry), each implemented in four portfolio variants and blended into composites, evaluated against three passive benchmarks. To the authors' knowledge the first application of factor investing to cryptocurrencies (Abstract; §V).

## Claim

The paper applies momentum, value, and carry factor portfolios to a universe of 11 cryptocurrencies and finds all three factors have explanatory power for shorter-horizon returns, with momentum "by far the most powerful individual factor" and blended multi-factor portfolios better still (Abstract; §V). The sample is daily data from April 28, 2013 (BTC, LTC) through September 2017, about 4 years and 4 months (§II.A, Table 1); portfolios rebalance weekly, are held 7 trading days, are run under all 7 possible rebalancing-day conventions, and the resulting return series are pooled (Table 1 note; §II.B). All portfolios are only 10% invested with 90% cash at zero assumed cash return, capping gross exposure to cryptocurrency risk at 10% (§II.B). In the pooled full-sample regression for 7-day returns (BASE_7), momentum, carry, and value coefficients are 0.119 (p=0.01), 0.050 (p=0), and 0.040 (p=0.083), adjusted R² 0.019 (Table 3). The complete-style composite factor portfolios earn annualized Sharpe ratios of 2.10 (equal-weighted) and 2.32 (risk-weighted) with alphas of 4.21% (p=0.008) and 6.07% (p=0.002) against the equal-weighted benchmark (Table 5). Value underperforms as a standalone factor but contributes through diversification; the blends beat momentum alone (Table 4, Table 5).

## Fit with our constraints

Instruments are spot cryptocurrencies only — 11 coins (BTC, DASH, DCR, DOGE, ETC, ETH, LTC, PIVX, XEM, XMR, ZEC) for which data were available on https://coinmetrics.io per September 2017 (Appendix 2). No derivatives are used; the paper states "a robust futures market is not available for cryptocurrencies" (Appendix 3) and "Cryptocurrencies cannot yet be shorted at scale" (§IV). The factor portfolios are long/short by construction (negative weights allowed), but the paper also constructs long-only "combined" portfolios (benchmark plus factor bets) in which negative currency weights are set to zero, "capturing the fact that outright short positions are challenging to establish" (§IV). Long and short legs are not reported separately — all results are portfolio-level, though the cross-sectional EW factor portfolios are "market neutral by construction" (top-half long, bottom-half short) and the paper notes cross-sectional RW portfolios need not be (§IV). Leverage: none; gross exposure is capped at 10% (sum of absolute weights equals 10% for factor portfolios, sum of weights equals 10% for benchmarks), 90% cash at zero return (§II.B). Replication would need daily price/market-value data, $-valued on-chain transaction volumes, coin issuance, and coins outstanding per currency from coinmetrics.io, plus maximum coin supply from www.cryptocompare.com (§II.A). Survivorship and delisting are not addressed: the universe is the 11 coins with data available on coinmetrics.io per September 2017, and the paper does not discuss whether failed or delisted coins are excluded (Appendix 2). Headline numbers are gross; costs are treated separately in Appendix 5 (see Test setup).

## Strategies

### Benchmarks ("what to hodl?", §II.C)

Three passive benchmarks, all 10% invested: **BM_EW** (equal fraction of the exposure budget to each available coin at rebalancing), **BM_CW** (weights proportional to market value, "the only portfolio that could simultaneously be held by all cryptocurrency investors in our universe"), and **BM_RW** (weights inversely proportional to trailing realized volatility, computed as standard deviation over a "telescoping" window of maximum possible length up to the rebalancing date based on daily overlapping 7-day returns with a 30-day initial burn-in). Annualized statistics (Table 2): BM_EW average 25.6%, std dev 10.0%, Sharpe 2.56, geometric mean 24.1%; BM_CW 9.9%, 6.8%, 1.46, 9.8%; BM_RW 21.6%, 8.8%, 2.45, 20.4%. BM_CW underperforms because BTC dominates market value while posting the second-lowest average return in the sample (§II.C). The 10% allocation is justified by a CRRA calibration: assuming cryptocurrency benchmarks deliver an equity-like Sharpe of 0.3 with current distributional characteristics, and risk aversion 2–3, utility-maximizing allocations were at or below 10%; the 10% figure was the upper end (footnote 5).

### Momentum (§II.D.1, §IV)

Raw momentum is the prior week's return for each currency (trailing 7 days). The economic frame is behavioral continuation — "the return we get if things keep changing the way they have" (Introduction). Standardization: z-scoring per currency (de-mean, divide by standard deviation), longitudinally using full-sample statistics in §III and only data available at portfolio construction in §IV; cross-sectional portfolios are additionally z-scored cross-sectionally at each point in time (§II.E, §IV). Two weighting schemes: EW (absolute weight 10%/n; complete portfolios: positive when the standardized factor is above zero, negative below; cross-sectional: top-half ranks positive, bottom-half negative) and RW (factor scores divided by the longest possible trailing volatility estimate per currency, then rescaled so absolute weights sum to 10%). Portfolios begin once at least four currencies are available (§IV). Best standalone variant: complete RW momentum, average return 19.8% annualized, Sharpe 1.70, alpha vs. CW benchmark 21.33% (p=0.001), IR 1.84 (Table 4). Momentum generates the highest trading volume, 80–90% annualized (Appendix 5). The authors note their signal is "much less sophisticated" than Rohrbach et al. (2017)'s but on a broader data set (Introduction).

### Value (§II.D.2, §IV)

Raw valuation metric is the ratio of current market value to the trailing 7-day average of $-valued on-chain transactions in the blockchain — the "Market to Transaction Value" (MTV) metric, named after its source coinmetrics.io; a high numeric value means the currency is expensive (footnote 9). Rationale: $-transaction volume proxies economic activity in the blockchain application, so market value relative to it should mean-revert. The paper shows MTV mean-reverts at much higher frequency than equity valuation cycles, which it credits to the asset class's "fast metabolism" (§II.D.2, Figure 2). Valuation data are not available for XMR (Table 1 note). Standardization is the same longitudinal/cross-sectional z-score scheme as momentum (§II.E, §IV). Standalone performance disappoints: "as a single factor, value disappoints — the alphas are positive throughout but far from statistically significant" (§IV); best alpha is 4.88% (p=0.148) for complete EW vs. CW benchmark (Table 4). Trading volume 30–50% annualized "given the fast mean reversion of our value metric" (Appendix 5).

### Carry (§II.D.3, §IV)

Raw carry is the negative of total coin issuance over the preceding 7 days divided by coins outstanding at the beginning of that 7-day period — i.e., negative protocol-driven "inflation" from mining. Rationale: if demand is unchanged, issuance dilutes nominal value, so "high carry" means low inflation; the authors frame it as closest to an expected-return metric (unlike value/momentum, which are indicative). It is therefore standardized by the volatility of the underlying currency return rather than z-scored (§II.E). Because carry is negative by construction, complete carry portfolios use a "panel z score" — after the volatility standardization, further standardizing with the mean and volatility of carry pooled across all currencies up to that point in time — so that attractive (less negative) carry is positive while structural cross-currency differences are preserved (footnote 15). Carry data are not available for XEM (Table 1 note). There is "some evidence of carry 'working'... including some statistically significant alphas" — e.g., complete EW carry alpha vs. CW benchmark 10.06% (p=0.022), Sharpe 2.19 (Table 4). Trading volume 10–25% annualized (Appendix 5).

### Composite and combined portfolios (§IV, Table 5)

Factors are blended at the portfolio level, not the signal level, following the literature. EW composites allocate 1/3 to each of the three factor portfolios; RW composites weight the three factor portfolios inversely to their trailing realized risk, rescaled to sum to one. All four blends have stronger characteristics than any individual factor; "the blends perform better than the momentum factor itself," taken as indirect evidence for value and carry (§IV). Complete EW composite: average 10.1%, Sharpe 2.10, alphas 4.21% (p=0.008) vs. EW, 8.20% (p=0.005) vs. CW, 4.79% (p=0.005) vs. RW. Complete RW composite: average 10.0%, Sharpe 2.32, alphas 6.07% (p=0.002), 8.00% (p=0.001), 6.20% (p=0.002). Cross-sectional blends are much weaker (Sharpe 0.27–1.30, mostly insignificant alphas) (Table 5). "Combined" portfolios add each EW factor composite to the EW benchmark (and RW to RW); negative currency weights are floored at zero. Combined complete EW: average 38.3%, Sharpe 2.91, alpha vs. CW 24.48% (p=0.021); combined complete RW: average 33.9%, Sharpe 2.91, alpha vs. CW 19.30% (p=0.011) (Table 5, right half). The paper attributes the superiority of complete over cross-sectional portfolios to the longitudinal dimension of factor signals (§IV).

## Test setup

Sample period runs from the earliest data (April 28, 2013, BTC and LTC) to the data snapshot per September 2017, with coins entering as their histories begin (Table 1); the paper is dated October 28, 2017. There is no separate out-of-sample or post-publication period — Section IV's point-in-time construction is the paper's "out of sample" analogue, and §III's regressions explicitly use hindsight (full-sample standardization). Number of instruments: 11 (9 usable for carry, 10 for value, per data availability). Data sources by name: https://coinmetrics.io for prices, on-chain transaction volumes, issuance, and coins outstanding; maximum coin supply from www.cryptocompare.com (Table 1). Survivorship/delisting: not addressed. Costs: headline returns are gross; Appendix 5 estimates that combined factor portfolios with ~30% annualized notional trading would incur at most 15 bps of trading costs under a conservative 0.2–0.5% of notional assumption for spreads plus taker fees (0.1–0.25% today), with market impact "assumed away"; a $10mn BTC transaction would move the combined USD order book 0.5–1% (bitcoinity.org). Benchmarks: the three described above, each used as the market proxy in performance-attribution regressions; alpha is the regression intercept with Newey-West heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation-consistent p-values, IR is alpha divided by residual standard deviation, both annualized (Table 4 note). Statistical significance is reported at the 10% level (bold) with shaded cells for significant positive alphas (Table 4 note).

## Results

The headline regression evidence (Table 3): at the 7-day horizon all three factors are significant with hypothesized signs (BASE_7: M 0.119 p=0.01; C 0.050 p=0; V 0.040 p=0.083). The "FIT" selection procedure (forward selection on univariate absolute correlation, re-estimating after each step, stopping when not all selected regressors are significant at 5%) drops carry at 7 days and instead introduces a negatively-signed 90-day value term (V_90 −0.156, p=0). Short-horizon momentum and value effects offset by negatively signed long-horizon (90-day) effects is a consistent pattern across horizons; carry is the weakest factor; longer-dated models look worse than shorter-dated ones (§III).

## Robustness

Appendix 4 reports: (i) non-overlapping periods under all 7 rebalancing-day definitions, using median estimates across the 7 versions; (ii) winsorization at the 2.5% level after standardization — this "led to a meaningful reduction in statistical significance for carry (p=0.35), and some weakening for value as well (p=0.16)"; (iii) dropping each of the 11 currencies one at a time and taking medians, to rule out a single coin driving results; (iv) a 90-day rather than 30-day burn-in for standardizations and risk estimates — "the most frequent reason" individual factor portfolios lost their significant alphas; (v) running the regression on ranked data, under which "the value effect became negligible, and entirely statistically insignificant." The combined multi-factor portfolios showed no deterioration under any check. Panel alternatives (currency fixed effects via F-tests, random effects via Breusch-Pagan) against pooled OLS produced essentially identical factor estimates except under fixed effects for the BASE_30 model, where momentum became positive and significant and value negative and significant; the paper kept pooled OLS.

## Specification search

The paper reports: 12 individual factor portfolios (3 factors × 2 weighting schemes × 2 perspectives), 4 composite blends, 4 combined portfolios, 8 regression models (4 horizons × BASE/FIT), all with results shown in Tables 3–5; plus 7 rebalancing-day conventions, 11 leave-one-currency-out versions, two burn-in lengths, winsorized and ranked regressions in the robustness appendix. All tested variants are reported; the only selection step is the described forward-selection FIT procedure in Table 3 (footnote 13).

## Limitations and future work

The authors' own (Conclusion): risk management applications added no value — risk-weighted portfolios did not outperform equal-weighted, which they suspect reflects fat-tailed returns defeating trailing-volatility risk estimates over a short sample; cryptocurrency portfolios pose unique challenges for multi-asset construction given extreme volatility (which they argue is an advantage for small allocations, not a flaw); and the sample is short and the universe fast-evolving — "we surmise that our initial study will be outdated in a year or two." The paper explicitly declines to endorse cryptocurrencies or factor strategies in them.

## Separable variants for our pipeline

- Momentum — prior 7-day return (§II.D.1), in EW and RW, cross-sectional and complete forms (§IV).
- Value / MTV — current market value ÷ trailing 7-day average of $-valued on-chain transactions (§II.D.2), same four portfolio forms.
- Carry — negative trailing 7-day coin issuance ÷ coins outstanding at period start (§II.D.3), volatility-standardized with panel z-score for complete forms; same four portfolio forms.
- Benchmarks — equal-weighted, market-value-weighted, risk-weighted (§II.C); each usable as a market proxy or long-only baseline.
- Composite blends — equal 1/3 allocation and inverse-volatility weighting of the three factor portfolios (§IV).
- Combined portfolios — benchmark plus factor composite with negative weights floored at zero (§IV); the paper's only long-only tradable specification.
