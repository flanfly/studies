Category: **comparison** — fourteen prediction models (12 machine learning methods plus 2 linear baselines) are built on a common factor set and evaluated against each other, on both statistical predictability and portfolio performance.

### Claim

The paper asks whether machine learning can predict weekly cross-sectional cryptocurrency returns, and which model class predicts best. Using a factor set of 25 predictors (17 cryptocurrency characteristic factors plus 8 macroeconomic factors) and weekly returns from January 1, 2014 to March 31, 2023, the authors train 12 ML models (RF, GBRT, KNN, NN1–NN5, PLS, LASSO, Ridge, ENet) plus OLS and an OLS restricted to market/size/momentum (OLS-3). Tree models dominate: random forest achieves the highest full-sample out-of-sample R² of 25.265%, versus 18.704% for the best neural network (NN4) and 3.901% for OLS (Table 3). Predictability increases with market cap, reaching an RF R² of 42.762% for mega-cap coins. SHAP analysis identifies the change rates of market-value-to-realized-value ratio (MRR), new addresses (NA), and active addresses (AA) as the three most important factors, all positively related to predicted returns (§3.3–3.4). In value-weighted portfolios, neural-network-based portfolios deliver the highest cumulative returns, with the NN5-based long-short portfolio posting the largest mean weekly return (37.066%) and the NN4-based long-only portfolio the largest mean (35.671%) (Table 5). The paper frames cryptocurrency returns as substantially more predictable by tree models than stock returns are, comparing against the U.S. (Gu et al. 2020) and Chinese (Leippold et al. 2022) stock markets (§3.2). No single t-statistic is reported for any headline return; statistical significance enters only through Diebold-Mariano forecast-accuracy tests (Table 4).

### Fit with our constraints

All instruments are cryptocurrencies (spot coins tracked by Coinmarketcap.com); returns are weekly. The long-short portfolios require a short leg (sell decile 1), and the paper itself notes that a short-selling mechanism is absent in the current cryptocurrency market and therefore treats long-short as diagnostic while also reporting long-only portfolios (§4). Long and short legs are not reported separately; only spread and long-only are given. The paper does not specify leverage; the long-short is described as zero-net-investment. Replication would need: daily OHLCV/market-cap/volume from Coinmarketcap.com, paid on-chain fundamentals from App.intotheblock.com, WIND financial-market data, and Google Trends series. All data are weekly-level for the factor predictors; the paper does not state a transaction-cost, financing-cost, or borrow-fee model, and Sharpe/mean figures appear to be gross.

### Strategies

The paper's units are the prediction models, which are then turned into decile portfolios. Each model is applied identically: at the end of each week, predict each cryptocurrency's next-week return from the 25 factors; sort into deciles; build value-weighted and equal-weighted long-short (decile 10 minus decile 1) and long-only (decile 10) portfolios (§4, footnote 3). Signal-to-trade gap is one week by construction (predict week t+1 from period-t information). Rebalance is weekly; holding period is one week. Turnover is not reported. The prediction target and conditioning setup are identical across models, so differences are purely the estimator.

The additive model is $r_{i,t+1} = \mathbb{E}_t[r_{i,t+1}] + \epsilon_{i,t+1}$ with $\mathbb{E}_t[r_{i,t+1}] = g(z_{i,t})$, $g$ unspecified (Eq. 1–2). Data are split chronologically into training (2014–2017), validation (2018–2020), and testing (2021–2023); hyperparameters are tuned on validation, and all inputs are Z-score standardized across the samples (§2.2). The testing sample never enters estimation or tuning, so reported R² are out-of-sample (Eq. 3).

**Linear and regularized models (OLS, OLS-3, PLS, Ridge, LASSO, ENet).** OLS regresses returns on all factors with an $l_2$ objective (Appendix A.1). OLS-3 restricts predictors to market, size, momentum. PLS constructs components maximizing covariance with the target (Appendix A.2). Ridge ($\rho=1$, $\alpha=0.8$), LASSO ($\rho=0$, $\alpha=1$), and ENet ($\alpha=0.5$, $l_1$ ratio 0.5) add penalties (Appendix A.3–A.5). Full-sample R²: OLS/PLS/Ridge all 3.901%, OLS-3 0.044%, LASSO/ENet −0.000%. The near-identity of OLS, PLS, and Ridge R² is read as evidence that all factors are informative and no dimension reduction is needed (§3.1.1).

**Tree models (RF, GBRT).** RF uses $B=100$ trees, depth $L=1\sim3$, $\sqrt{P}$ features considered per split (Appendix A.6). GBRT uses $B=100$ trees, depth $L=1\sim3$, $n=50$ features, learning rate $\nu=0.01$ (Appendix A.7). RF is the best full-sample model overall (R² = 25.265%), marginally ahead of GBRT (24.911%); both exceed 40% on mega-cap coins (42.762% and 41.146%) (Table 3). The authors attribute tree superiority to their capture of nonlinear interactions among predictors, and cite the same pattern being absent in stock markets (§3.2).

**Neural networks (NN1–NN5).** Fully connected networks with layer widths 32 / 32-16 / 32-16-8 / 32-16-8-4 / 32-16-8-4-2 neurons, sigmoid activation in every layer, AMSGrad optimizer ($\alpha=0.001$, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$), batch size 16, early stopping after 20 non-improving validation epochs and a 1000-epoch cap (Appendix A.9). NN4 is best at full sample (18.704%) and mega-cap (36.090%); NN5 is marginally lower (18.478%). Adding depth helps only through the fourth hidden layer (§3.1.1).

**KNN.** $k=10$ nearest neighbors (Appendix A.8); R² = 15.831% full sample, 21.762% mega-cap (Table 3), between linear and tree/NN models.

### Test setup

Sample period is January 1, 2014 to March 31, 2023, chosen because pre-2014 markets had limited liquidity (§2.1). The training/validation/testing split (2014–2017 / 2018–2020 / 2021–2023) is the only time partition; the testing window serves as the out-of-sample period, and no additional post-publication or separate holdout is described. The universe is all cryptocurrencies on Coinmarketcap.com with complete price, market-cap, and volume data, excluding coins with market cap below $10,000,000; the market-return formula sums over 940 cryptocurrencies (Appendix B.7). Survivorship is addressed by noting Coinmarketcap lists active and defunct coins; delisting handling is not described. Data sources by name: Coinmarketcap.com (daily trading data), App.intotheblock.com (on-chain characteristic fundamentals), WIND (financial market indices), Google Trends (search data), policyuncertainty.com (EPU and TEU indices). Transaction and financing costs are not specified; reported Sharpe and mean returns appear gross. The benchmark is the equal-weighted context of the in-sample factors and, for market comparison, the U.S. and Chinese stock markets via Gu et al. (2020) and Leippold et al. (2022).

### Results

Out-of-sample weekly R² (Table 3) and value-weighted portfolio performance (Table 5) are the two reported result blocks. R² rises with cap segment for every model; tree and NN models always beat linear ones. In portfolios, machine-learning portfolios dominate linear ones; PLS posts the largest long-short Sharpe (286.080) and smallest std (18.955), while NN5 has the largest long-short mean (37.066) and NN4 the largest long-only mean (35.671).

The paper reports no t-statistics on any portfolio return; significance is only via Diebold-Mariano forecast tests (Table 4). Commonly reported metrics absent entirely: no net-of-cost returns, no turnover, no capacity estimates.

The authors conclude that machine learning, and tree models in particular, predict weekly cryptocurrency returns far better than linear benchmarks and better than the same models predict stocks; that MRR, NA, and AA drive predictions positively; and that neural-network portfolios earn the highest cumulative returns while tree portfolios are close second, with PLS the best variance-minimising long-short choice (§3–4). They note that long-short longs are unimplementable because crypto lacks shorting, so they emphasise long-only results (§4). During the November 2021–June 2022 market crash, neural-network and tree portfolios' cumulative returns rose against the market trend (§4, Figure 5).

### Robustness

The principal robustness work is the cap-segment split: every model is re-evaluated on mega-cap (>$1B weekly), large-cap ($100M–$1B), and mid-cap ($10M–$100M) coins, with the pattern that predictability concentrates in mega-caps and degrades into mid-caps, and Diebold-Mariano tests reproduced for each segment (Appendix D, Tables D.1–D.3). Equal-weighted portfolios are reported in Appendix E (Table E.1) alongside the value-weighted tables, and the paper notes equal-weighted results differ in which NN dominates. There is no alternative-universe, alternative-frequency, alternative-lookback, or delayed-execution robustness section, and no sensitivity analysis beyond the model hyperparameter grid implicit in validation tuning.

### Specification search

The paper reports 14 prediction models × 4 cap segments (full, mega, large, mid) for R², DM tests for each segment, and 14 models × 2 weighting schemes (value-, equal-weighted) × 2 portfolio types (long-short, long-only) for performance. All of these are tabulated; the paper does not report a broader grid of hyperparameter values than the final selected ones, and it does not report results for factor subsets beyond the all-factors and three-factor (OLS-3) cases.

### Limitations and future work

The authors state that long-short portfolios are difficult to implement because the cryptocurrency market lacks a short-selling mechanism, which is why they also study long-only portfolios (§4). They do not discuss transaction costs, capacity, or data availability constraints as limitations; the concluding remarks frame the contribution as guidance for investors, traders, and financial analysts (§5).

### Separable variants for our pipeline

This is a comparison paper, so the list is essentially the strategies again. Individually testable signals/estimators:

- Random forest forecast using the 25-factor set, weekly rebalance, decile portfolios (Table 3; Appendix A.6).
- GBRT forecast, same setup (Table 3; Appendix A.7).
- Neural networks NN1–NN5, each with its fixed architecture (Table 3; Appendix A.9).
- KNN forecast, k=10 (Table 3; Appendix A.8).
- Factor set as predictors: the 17 characteristic factors (Appendix B) and 8 macroeconomic factors (Appendix C), individually rankable by SHAP importance (Figure 2).
