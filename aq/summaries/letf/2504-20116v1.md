*Category: empirical (market phenomenon with a theoretical framework and portfolio-style validation, not a tradable-strategy backtest).*

### Claim

The paper argues that long-term leveraged-ETF (LETF) performance is driven not chiefly by the well-documented "volatility drag" but by return autocorrelation and return dynamics. In markets with independent returns, LETFs exhibit a positive *expected compounding effect* on their target multiples; under positive serial correlation (momentum) trends enhance LETF returns, while negative serial correlation (mean reversion) induces underperformance. The paper develops a unified framework spanning an i.i.d. discrete-time benchmark, an AR(1) model, an AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) model, and a continuous-time regime-switching model, and validates it against roughly twenty years (about 7,800 days, January 1993–December 2023 for SPY; QQQ from 2010) of SPY and QQQ data (§1, §4). Headline insight: daily rebalancing is optimal in momentum regimes while weekly/monthly rebalancing mitigates losses in mean-reverting regimes; LETFs outperform in trending markets and underperform in sideways markets (Abstract, §3.2.2, §4.2).

The central object is the compounding effect (Definition 2.1, eq. 1):
$$\mathbf{CE}_n := R_n^{\text{LETF},\beta} - \beta R_n^{\text{ETF}},$$
where $R_n^{\text{LETF},\beta}=\prod_{t=1}^n(1+X_t^{\text{LETF}})-1$ and $R_n^{\text{ETF}}=\prod_{t=1}^n(1+X_t)-1$, with leverage $\beta \in \mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$ ($\beta\in\{-2,-1,2,3\}$; negative $\beta$ = inverse ETFs). LETF daily returns are modeled as $X_t^{\text{LETF}}=\beta X_t - f + e_t$, where $f$ is a daily fee including expense ratio and transaction costs ($f\approx\frac{1}{252}(f_{\text{expense}}+f_{\text{transaction}})$) and $e_t$ is zero-mean tracking error (eq. §2.2).

### Fit with our constraints

Instrument classes are equity LETFs and their underlying SPY/QQQ ETFs. The main signal is a property of leverage β, not a cross-sectional signal; the "arbitrage-like" portfolios (long one unit of LETF, short β units of ETF, capture $R_n^{\text{LETF}}-\beta R_n^{\text{ETF}}=\mathbf{CE}_n$) require a short leg, which is short stock/ETF — the paper does not discuss expressing the short in futures or perpetuals (§3.1.2). Long and short legs are treated as a single portfolio; leverage is fixed at β by construction. Data replication needs daily (or weekly/monthly) adjusted close prices for SPY, QQQ and the listed LETFs, plus the six identified market regimes. No transaction-cost or financing model beyond the fee term $f$ is specified.

### Strategies

The paper describes no independently stated tradable strategy; its unit of analysis is the compounding effect and the conditions under which it is positive or negative. Each subsection below is a distinct regime/return model the paper uses.

**i.i.d. discrete-time benchmark.** With $X_t$ independent, mean $\mu$, leverage β, βμ > −1, Lemma 3.1 gives $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{CE}_n]=[(1+\beta\mu)^n-1]-\beta[(1+\mu)^n-1]\ge0$ for all $n\ge1$ (§3.1). Economically this is a Jensen-convexity effect; since $\beta(\beta-1)>0$, more leverage amplifies it. Monte Carlo with 100,000 one-year paths, daily returns ~N(μ, σ²), annualized μ from −20% to +20%, σ = 1%, shows positive E[CE] for β ∈ {−2,−1,2,3}, larger as |β| grows and as |μ| moves from zero; changes in σ (0.5%, 1%, 1.5%) and rebalancing frequency (daily/weekly/monthly) leave E[CE] nearly unchanged (Figs 1–3, §3.1.1).

**AR(1) model.** $X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$, $c=0$, $|\phi|<1$, $\mathbb{E}[X_tX_{t-1}]=\phi\sigma^2/(1-\phi^2)$. Lemma 3.2: φ > 0 ⟹ E[CE_n] > 0; φ < 0 ⟹ E[CE_n] < 0 (§3.2.1). Monte Carlo with 10,000 paths over φ ∈ [−0.9, 0.9] shows sign-dependence of E[CE]; volatility amplifies it for φ > 0 and worsens it for φ < 0; daily rebalancing is best for φ > 0 while weekly reduces and monthly nearly eliminates the negative effect for φ < 0 (Figs 4–6, §3.2.2). No management metrics (turnover, Sharpe) are reported.

**AR(1)-GARCH(1,1) estimated model.** Params fit by MLE to daily returns of SPY, SSO, SPXL, SDS, SH (Feb 2010–Dec 2023, Table 1). Estimates show mild mean-reversion (negative AR(1), e.g., SPY $\phi=-0.0490^{***}$), strong volatility persistence (SPY $\alpha=0.1747^{***}$, $\beta=0.7969^{***}$, so α+β ≈ 0.97). Simulating 10,000 one-year (252-day) paths gives E[CE_{252}]: SSO(2x) 0.0744 (sd 0.1844), SPXL(3x) 0.2443 (0.6383), SDS(−2x) 0.1664 (0.2756), SH(−1x) 0.0597 (0.1063) (Table 2); QQQ analogues (Table 3): QLD 0.1206 (0.2564), TQQQ 0.4027 (0.9647), QID 0.2483 (0.3659), SQQQ 0.4560 (0.6313). Conclusion: volume clustering (α+β) dominates autocorrelation as a driver in high-volatility regimes; autocorrelation governs low-volatility regimes (§3.2.4).

**Continuous-time regime-switching model.** $dS_t=\mu_{Z_t}S_t\,dt+\sigma_{Z_t}S_t\,dW_t$ with Markov chain $Z_t$; LETF obeys $dL_t/L_t=\beta\,dS_t/S_t-f\,dt$. Theorem 3.1 approximates $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{CE}_t]\approx\sum_{j=1}^M\pi_j(t)[\exp((\beta\mu_j-f)t)-\beta\exp(\mu_j t)]+(\beta-1)$ with occupation measure $\pi_j(t)$; Theorem 3.2 gives the sign condition $\sum_j\pi_j(t)\Phi_j(t;\beta,f)\gtrless 1-\beta$; Corollary 3.1 shows E[CE_t] ≥ 0 in a single constant regime with f = 0 (§3.3). In regime mixing, even with each Φ_j ≥ 0, E[CE_t] can go negative (Remark 3.5).

### Test setup

Empirical validation uses adjusted daily closes of SPY and QQQ and actual LETFs. Six defined market regimes: Financial Crisis (Oct 2007–Mar 2009), Post-Crisis Recovery (Apr 2009–Mar 2013), Sideways Market (Feb 2014–Sep 2015), COVID-19 Pandemic (Feb 2020–Mar 2020), Post-COVID Recovery (Apr 2020–Dec 2021), 2022 Bear Market (§4.2). Tickers: SPY family SDS(−2x), SH(−1x), SSO(+2x), SPXL(+3x); QQQ family SQQQ(−3x), QID(−2x), QLD(+2x), TQQQ(+3x). Survivership/delisting handling is not addressed. Headline compounding figures are gross of fees for synthetic LETFs; the paper states that transaction costs and fees account for roughly 0.8–1.0 percentage points per annum of observed underperformance, with bid-ask spreads, slippage and tracking error adding more (§4.2.2). No benchmark index or factor model is applied.

The authors conclude Tables 4 and 5 are directionally consistent: CE is positive in trending regimes (Post-Crisis and Post-COVID Recovery including downward-trending crisis for positive β), negative in the awkward Sideways and high-volatility windows, and larger in magnitude for |β| = 3. H1 ("LETFs outperform in strongly trending markets") is confirmed; H2 ("negative in mean-reverting markets") holds broadly but admits exceptions (inverse SQQQ showed small positive CE in the 2014–15 sideways window on net upward drift in QQQ); H3 ("CE magnitude scales with leverage and volatility") is confirmed. The paper does not report t-statistics, Sharpe ratios, or cost-adjusted net figures anywhere.

### Robustness

Beyond the SPY sample, the AR(1)-GARCH estimation and simulations are rerun on QQQ and its LETFs, showing larger CE magnitudes attributed to QQQ's higher realized volatility (Table 3). The empirical regime analysis is repeated for QQQ in parallel (Tables 4–5). A rolling-window analysis (60-, 90-, 120-day windows, 2009/2010–2024, Figs 7–12) shows time-varying CE and rolling AR(1) coefficients (Figs 13–18), with positive CE aligning with elevated AR(1) (2010–11, 2016–17, early 2020) and decaying CE in low/negative-autocorrelation stretches (late 2015, mid-2022) (§4.3). Alternative return specifications (regime-switching extensions, rough volatility) are listed as future work, not tested.

### Specification search

The paper reports examining, by Monte Carlo: leverage β ∈ {−2,−1,2,3} combined with mean μ sweeps (−20% to +20% annualized), volatility 0.5%/1%/1.5%, rebalancing daily/weekly/monthly, AR coefficient φ ∈ [−0.9, 0.9], and six named market regimes — but reports figures only for selected combinations (e.g., volatility results shown only for β = 2 in the independent case, §3.1.1 fn. 3). Counts of variant runs are not tabulated; 100,000 (independent) and 10,000 (AR(1)/AR-GARCH) paths per configuration are stated.

### Limitations and future work

The authors note a full decomposition of autocorrelation versus volatility-clustering effects remains future work; that in the AR-GARCH setting the sign of E[CE_n] is not guaranteed in theory so arbitrage-like portfolios require careful calibration; and that future specifications could include rough-volatility or non-Markovian regime-switching models to capture finer return dynamics (§3.2.4, §3.2.5, §5).

### Separable variants for our pipeline

- Compounding-effect signal E[CE_n] under i.i.d. returns, eq. (2), Lemma 3.1 (§3.1).
- Compounding-effect sign under AR(1) autocorrelation, Lemma 3.2 (§3.2.1) — the conditioning variable is the sign of tracked φ.
- Compounding-effect sign under continuous-time regime occupation weights π_j(t), Theorems 3.1–3.2 (§3.3.1).
- LETF rebalancing-frequency choice (daily vs weekly vs monthly) conditional on detected momentum or mean-reversion (§3.2.2).
- The "arbitrage-like" long-LETF/short-β-ETF portfolio capturing CE (§3.1.2) — noted here as the paper's only portfolio construction, not a validated strategy.
