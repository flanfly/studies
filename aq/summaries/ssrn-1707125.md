Category: **modification** with a comparison component. The baseline is the pairs trading algorithm of Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst ("GGR", 2006) plus the portfolio variants of Do and Faff (2010), and the change applied to all of them is an explicit, time-varying transaction-cost model (commissions + market impact + short-selling fees) subtracted from gross returns. The paper then compares the cost-adjusted pairs portfolios against Hameed, Huang and Mian's (2010) intra-industry reversal strategy under the same cost treatment. The baselines are precisely identified by citation (GGR 2006; Do and Faff 2010; Hameed et al. 2010).

## Claim

Over the period July 1963–June 2009 in the US equity market, pairs trading remains mildly profitable after commissions, market impact and short-selling fees, but only for portfolios of well-matched pairs formed within refined (Fama-French 48) industry groups. Before costs, the 29 pairs portfolios generate monthly excess returns averaging 93 bps (range 70–115 bps); after costs this falls to an average of 12 bps (range −7 to +35 bps), and the GGR baseline portfolio becomes unprofitable (§IV, Tables 4–5). The four best after-cost portfolios (24, 25, 27, 29) post an average excess return of 28 bps per month (3.37% p.a.) and risk-adjusted alphas of about 30 bps per month (Table 5, §IV; Table 7), or 24 bps when restricted to the top 3 size deciles (Table 9, Panel A). Both pairs trading and the intra-industry reversal benchmark are "largely unprofitable in the period post 2002" (Abstract; §IV, Table 8). Pairs trading exhibits a lower-risk, lower-return profile than intra-industry reversal, whose after-cost return is 65 bps/month with roughly double the standard deviation (2.35% vs ~0.93%).

## Fit with our constraints

Instrument class is single-name US common equity (CRSP share codes 10 and 11); nothing in the strategy is expressible in futures or perpetuals, since the edge is the idiosyncratic relative mispricing between two specific stocks. The strategy requires a short leg — each pair trade shorts the relatively "dear" stock — so it cannot run long-only; the short side is not the source of alpha in any reported decomposition, but the paper reports long-leg and short-leg returns separately only in the market-impact analysis (Table 3: long leg +32 bps and short leg −21 bps on the day after divergence), not in the portfolio return tables, which give combined long-short portfolio returns only. No leverage is described; portfolios are (implicitly) dollar-neutral, equal-weighted across pairs — the construction "monthly marked-to-market payoffs to the pair portfolio, divided by the number of pairs" (§II) does not explicitly state leg sizing, and that step is ambiguous in the paper. Replication needs: daily CRSP price/volume/return data, NYSE size breakpoints, SIC-based Fama-French 48 industry classification, the Jones (2002) commission series, ITG (2008, 2010) commission reports, and D'Avolio's (2002) security-lending data for the short fee. Delisting handling is not described as such; pairs with invalid or missing returns during the trading period are assigned zero returns (§II).

## Strategies

All 29 pairs portfolios share one mechanical core and differ only in matching and selection; the paper's contribution is the cost model applied to them and the comparison with intra-industry reversal. All monthly excess-return series are the equally weighted average of six return series staggered by one month, formed by repeating the process every month (§II).

### GGR (2006) baseline — portfolio 1

**Signal (verbatim mechanics, §II "The Baseline Portfolio").** For each stock in the universe, a partner is chosen that minimizes the normalized price spread during a twelve-month formation period, where the normalized price is the total return index, inclusive of dividends, scaled to start at $1 at the beginning of the formation period. "Top pairs in terms of the lowest sum of squared differences in the normalized prices (hereafter, 'SSD')" are traded in the subsequent six-month trading period. A trade opens "whenever its normalized spread diverges by two historical standard deviations"; it is closed "at the first reversal of the spread or at the end of the trading period if reversal never occurs"; pairs completing a roundtrip may trade again within the period. The baseline portfolio is the equally weighted top 20 SSD pairs. The paper applies the 20% institutional-commission discount, the market-impact schedule, and a 1% p.a. short fee (below). Gross: mean 0.0085/month (t = 9.72), Sharpe 0.76; net: mean −0.0004 (t = −0.82), Sharpe −0.05 (Tables 4, 5) — the baseline "loses its profitability" (§IV-V).

### The cost model (the delta)

Applied to every strategy: two roundtrips of commissions plus market impact per completed pair trade, and a short-selling fee of a constant 1% per annum payable over the life of each open pair trade (§III). Commissions: Jones' (2002) annual NYSE series, discounted 20% to reflect institutional long-short funds, augmented with ITG (2008, 2010) estimates for 2005–2009 (10 bps in 2005, 9/7/8/9 bps in 2006–2009) and an interpolated 10 bps for 2001–2004; average 34 bps over 1963–2009, peaking at 72 bps (institutional) in 1974 (Table 2). Market impact: estimated ex post from actual price movements after divergence signals — on average the spread jumps from 4.41% to 7.56% on the divergence day, then narrows over two days; long-leg returns +32/+15 bps and short-leg −21/−9 bps on days T+1/T+2 (Table 3); assigned cost is 30 bps one-way for 1963–1988 and 20 bps for 1989–2009, giving average one-way cost (commission + impact) of 60 bps full sample, 81 bps 1963–1988, 33 bps 1989–2009, 28 bps 2003–2009 (§III). Execution is therefore assumed within two days of the divergence/convergence signal; no cost assumption for same-day execution is given. Rationale for the change: GGR's bid-ask-spread proxy omitted commissions, which matter because a pair trade is two roundtrips (§I).

### SSD+NZC portfolios matched within Fama-French 48 industries — portfolios 19–27

Same mechanics, with two refinements drawn from Do and Faff (2010): pair matching restricted to within Fama-French (1997) 48-industry groups (SIC-based), and selection by "NZC" — the number of zero crossings of the normalized price spread over the formation period, a measure of reversal frequency — used in conjunction with SSD. Portfolios take top-N SSD pairs, or pairs 11–30 (dropping the lowest-SSD, "too tightly co-moving" pairs whose 2σ divergence is too small to cover costs), or the intersection of SSD vigintile/decile with the top NZC vigintile/decile (Table 1). Portfolios 10–18 are identical but matched within the four S&P major sectors (Utilities, Financials, Transport, Industrials; CRSP HSICMG ranges 49, 60–67, 40–47, 15–39). After costs, all portfolios combining SSD and NZC (6, 14, 15, 17, 23–29) are profitable, mostly at 1% significance, while SSD-only portfolios mostly lose (Table 5). Portfolios 24 (2nd SSD vigintile ∩ 20th NZC vigintile, within FF48) and 27 (2nd SSD decile ∩ 10th NZC decile, within FF48) are two of the three best net Sharpe ratios (0.32 and 0.23; Table 5) and have after-cost Model-1 alphas of 0.0039 (t = 5.77) and 0.0030 (t = 5.59) respectively (Table 7).

### Single-industry portfolios — Utilities (28) and Banks (29)

The two Do and Faff (2010) portfolios: top-20 NZC pairs among the top-50 SSD pairs formed within utilities and banks stocks respectively (Table 1). After costs: Banks mean 0.0025 (t = 3.13), Utilities 0.0013 (t = 2.59) per month (Table 5). The Banks portfolio jumps from rank 27 gross to rank 8 net because it trades least: 2.2 trades per pair per six-month trading period versus an average of 2.9 (§IV; the average across portfolios is "unreported" in tabular form).

### Intra-industry reversal (Hameed et al. 2010) — comparator

A monthly double sort: stocks are ranked into Market Winners (prior-month return top quintile), Market Losers (bottom quintile), Market Neutral (middle 60%); an independent sort on the same prior-month return within Fama-French 48 industries identifies Industry Winners/Losers/Neutral; the strategy buys the intersection of Industry Losers × Market Neutral and shorts Industry Winners × Market Neutral. Gross excess return 1.91%/month (t = 19.10) with standard deviation 2.35% — the paper's variant slightly exceeds Hameed et al.'s 1.82% because of the finer 48-industry classification and a slightly different sample (§IV, footnote 11). Net: 65 bps/month (t = 6.52), Sharpe 0.28 (Table 5). Correlation with the pairs portfolios is only ~0.13 (§IV). Trading intensity is one roundtrip per month by construction, versus once every 2.7 months for pairs portfolios; the strategy is therefore more cost-sensitive — under the top-3-decile restriction its net return collapses to 2 bps and its alpha to 9 bps (insignificant), versus 24 bps alpha for the top-4 pairs portfolios (Table 9, Panel A; §IV).

## Test setup

Sample: daily CRSP files, share codes 10 and 11, July 1962–June 2009 (formation begins July 1962; return series run July 1963–June 2009). Sample end June 2009 versus paper date 31 January 2011; no post-publication or out-of-sample period is reported. Screens: exclude stocks in the bottom size decile (NYSE breakpoints), stocks with closing prices below $5 during the formation year, stocks with one or more days of no trade in the formation period (via volume), and stocks with invalid prices/returns in the formation period; pairs with invalid or missing prices/returns during the trading period are assigned zero returns (§II, "Stock sample"). Membership is reconstructed from formation-period data only, so it is point-in-time feasible, though the paper does not use the phrase. Data sources: CRSP; Jones (2002) commissions; ITG (2008, 2010); D'Avolio (2002) for shorting; Fama-French factors, momentum, and Pastor-Stambaugh liquidity (the WRDS liquidity innovation series is used to avoid spurious regression); idiosyncratic volatility from the Campbell-Lettau-Malkiel-Xu (2001) decomposition. Survivorship/delisting: delisting treatment is not described; the trailing-period filters are the only missing-price handling. Transaction costs: as in the cost model above — headline net figures are net of commissions, market impact and short fees; no financing or borrow-cost-above-1% modeling. Benchmark factor models: Model 1 = FF3 + momentum + marketwide short-term reversal (GGR's specification); Model 2 = FF3 + momentum + intra-industry reversal; Model 3 = FF3 + momentum + Pastor-Stambaugh liquidity; Model 4 = FF3 + momentum + aggregate idiosyncratic volatility (Tables 6, 7). All t-stats are Newey-West with six lags; Sharpe z-stats use Lo (2002) standard errors.

## Robustness

Reported: the top-3-size-decile universe (Table 9, Panel A) — top-4 alpha falls from 32 to 24 bps, intra-industry alpha becomes insignificant (9 bps), confirming the reversal effect is concentrated in less liquid names; a 10 bps lower one-way cost assumption for large caps (Table 9, Panel B) — top-4 alpha rises to 37 bps and the intra-industry to 34 bps; four sub-periods within 1989–2009 (Table 8), which honestly shows the effect largely confined to the 2000–2002 bear market; four factor models (Tables 6, 7) — Model 2 reduced but never eliminated gross alphas; a GARCH(1,1) volatility comparison confirming pairs trading's lower risk profile (Figure 1). The paper also documents, via convergence statistics, that the 2007–2009 bear market offered weaker opportunities than 2000–2002: 67% of top-4 pairs converged vs 55%, weighted average profit per pair 1.23 percentage points lower, average divergence 8.24% vs 10.00% (§IV).

## Specification search

Twenty-nine pairs portfolios are examined (nine each of unconditional, major-sector, and FF-industry matching, plus utilities and banks), all of which are reported in full in Tables 4–8 and Table 9 for two universes and two cost scenarios; four factor models on gross and net returns; four sub-periods. The 29 formations are pre-specified from the authors' prior work and GGR — the paper flags concern about data snooping and states it deliberately retained GGR's formation/trading durations, trigger rule and matching scheme (footnote 5) — and performance is reported for all of them, not selected winners.

## Limitations and future work

The authors' own: "limits to arbitrage" risks are not accounted for in the regressions — fundamental risk of permanent divergence (Shleifer and Vishny 1997), synchronization risk (Abreu and Brunnermeier 2002), and the crowd-trade/forced-unwind risk highlighted by Stein (2009) and the August 2007 quant crisis (Khandani and Lo 2007) — so pre-2003 profits "might be simply compensation for arbitrageurs bearing these risks," and near-zero post-2002 alphas could reflect steeper losses (§IV, Discussion). They propose that future evaluation of arbitrage strategies draw on asset-based style factors in the spirit of Fung and Hsieh (2002, 2004). They also leave open why post-2002 mispricings shrank: market efficiency versus a more uniform (financial rather than tech-led) bear market (§IV).

## Separable variants for our pipeline

All are descriptive, from the paper:

- GGR baseline: top-20 lowest-SSD pairs, unconditionally matched (§II, Table 1; portfolios 1, 10, 19).
- Top-50 pairs and skipped-lowest-SSD variants (pairs 11–30, 11–60) across the three matching schemes (Table 1; portfolios 2–4, 11–13, 20–22).
- SSD∩NZC vigintile/decile intersection portfolios, three matching schemes (Table 1; portfolios 5–9, 14–18, 23–27).
- Utilities-only (28) and bank-only (29) single-industry portfolios (Table 1).
- Intra-industry reversal double sort on prior-month return (Market/Industry quintiles × Fama-French 48) (§IV, Tables 4–5).
