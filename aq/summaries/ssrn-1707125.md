**Category: comparison** — 29 pairs-trading portfolios plus Hameed, Huang and Mian's (2010) intra-industry reversal strategy are evaluated against each other, before and after a reconstructed trading-cost series. It is also a modification paper in substance: every strategy is the previously published GGR (2006) or Hameed et al. (2010) construction with the single change that commissions, market impact, and short-selling fees are subtracted.

## Claim

The paper re-examines US equity pairs trading over July 1963–June 2009 after explicitly subtracting all three components of direct trading costs: two roundtrips of commissions and market impact per completed pair trade, plus a short-selling fee paid over the life of the trade (Introduction). On average, pairs trading is unprofitable net of costs: monthly excess returns fall from 93 bps gross to 12 bps net across the 29 portfolios (Tables 4–5). Portfolios of better-matched pairs formed within refined industry groups remain mildly profitable: the four best (24, 25, 27, 29) post an average net excess return of 28 bps/month, or 3.37% annualized, and a risk-adjusted alpha of about 30 bps/month (Tables 5, 7; §IV). Restricted to the top 30% of stocks by market capitalization, the same four portfolios deliver an average alpha of 24 bps/month (Table 9 Panel A). Pairs trading has a lower-risk, lower-return profile than Hameed et al.'s (2010) intra-industry contrarian strategy, whose net excess return is 65 bps/month with roughly double the standard deviation (Tables 4–5). Both forms of contrarian investing are largely unprofitable post-2002 (Table 8; §IV Discussion).

## Fit with our constraints

Instruments are US common stocks (CRSP share codes 10 and 11); no futures, options, or perp instruments. Every strategy requires a short leg, and it is a specific stock (the "dear" pair member, or the industry winner), not a market index — it cannot be expressed in futures or perpetuals except as market-exposure hedging. Long- and short-leg returns are reported separately only in the execution-cost analysis of Table 3 (long leg +32 bps, short leg −21 bps on the day after divergence, full-sample averages); all portfolio-level returns in Tables 4–9 are combined long–short excess returns, so a spread that is entirely a short-leg effect cannot be identified from the reported numbers. Leverage is not specified. Replication needs daily CRSP data (1962–2009), NYSE size breakpoints, Fama-French and momentum factors, the Pastor-Stambaugh (2003) liquidity innovation series, Jones (2002) commission series, ITG commission reports, and the 1% p.a. loan-fee assumption. Microcaps and penny stocks are excluded (bottom size decile and sub-$5 screens, §II "Stock sample").

## Strategies

### 1. GGR (2006) baseline pairs trading — Portfolio 1 (baseline plus cost delta)

Baseline: Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst (2006), as described at §II "The Baseline Portfolio": over a 12-month formation period, each stock is matched to the partner minimizing the normalized price spread, where the normalized price is the total return index inclusive of dividends scaled to start at $1 at the beginning of the formation period. Pairs are ranked by the sum of squared differences (SSD) of normalized prices; the top 20 are traded in the following 6-month trading period. A long–short position opens whenever the normalized spread diverges by two historical standard deviations and closes at the first reversal of the spread or at the end of the trading period; pairs completing a roundtrip may be re-traded within the period. The portfolio is equally weighted across the top 20 pairs; overlapping portfolios start every month, giving six staggered return series whose equally weighted average is reported (§II "Construction of Return Time Series"). The change from GGR: two roundtrips of time-varying commissions (Table 2 series) plus market impact (30 bps one-way 1963–1988, 20 bps 1989–2009) per completed trade, and a constant 1% p.a. loan fee over the life of each trade (§III). Gross mean excess return 85 bps/month (t = 9.72, Sharpe 0.76, Table 4); net mean −4 bps/month (t = −0.82, Sharpe −0.05, Table 5). The authors frame the mechanism as exploitation of law-of-one-price violations by a market-neutral arbitrage strategy; they name no specific counterparty, but describe profits as compensation for providing liquidity and for temporary mispricings that revert (§IV "Risk Characteristics", citing Engelberg, Gao and Jagannathan 2009).

### 2. Refined pairs portfolios — Portfolios 2–29

These vary the baseline along three principles motivated by Do and Faff (2010) and the authors' critique of the baseline (§II): (i) matching within industries — the 4 S&P major sectors (portfolios 10–18) or the 48 Fama-French (1997) industries (portfolios 19–27), versus unconditional matching (1–9); (ii) adding the number of zero crossings (NZC) of the normalized spread over the formation period as a second matching metric, taking intersections of SSD and NZC vigintiles/deciles (portfolios 5–9, 14–18, 23–27); and (iii) skipping the lowest-SSD pairs whose 2-sigma divergences are too small to cover costs (portfolios 3–4, 12–13, 21–22; and the 2nd/3rd SSD vigintile portfolios). Portfolios 28 and 29 are single-industry portfolios — all-Utilities and all-Banks — formed as the top 20 NZC pairs among the top 50 SSD pairs (Table 1). Costs as in Strategy 1. Net monthly excess returns range −7 bps to 35 bps (Table 5); the profitable portfolios (6, 14, 15, 17, 23–29) are exactly those combining SSD and NZC. Top-4 portfolios 24, 25, 27, 29: net mean 28 bps/month (Table 5), alphas of 27–34 bps/month across Models 1–4 (Table 7). The Banks portfolio has the lowest trade count, 2.2 trades per pair per 6-month period versus an average of 2.9 (untabulated, §IV "Relative Performance"). Turnover: the pairs portfolios turn over once every 2.7 months (footnote 12, unreported statistic).

### 3. Intra-industry reversal — Hameed, Huang and Mian (2010)

Baseline: Hameed et al. (2010), reconstructed by the authors. Stocks are independently double-sorted on prior-month returns: Market Winners/Losers/Neutral are top quintile, bottom quintile, and middle 60% relative to the market; an analogous sort within the 48 Fama-French (1997) industries defines Industry Winners/Losers/Neutral. The strategy buys the intersection of Industry Losers and Market Neutral and shorts the intersection of Industry Winners and Market Neutral, rebalanced monthly (Table 4 note). The delta from the original: net-of-cost evaluation plus the authors' 48-industry (versus 12-sector) classification, which they note raises the gross return to 1.91%/month versus 1.82% reported by Hameed et al. (footnote 11). One roundtrip per month by construction. Gross mean 1.91%/month (t = 19.10, Sharpe 0.81, Table 4); net mean 65 bps/month (t = 6.52, Sharpe 0.28, Table 5); on the top-3-decile universe, net alpha 9 bps and raw return 2 bps/month (Table 9 Panel A).

**What the comparison establishes.** After costs, the baseline GGR portfolio is unprofitable while roughly half the refined portfolios and the intra-industry strategy remain profitable; pairs trading offers much lower return and risk than intra-industry reversal (GARCH(1,1) conditional volatility, Figure 1); and the intra-industry effect is concentrated in less liquid stocks, while pairs trading survives (weakly) in the top 3 size deciles. Correlation between the pairs portfolios and intra-industry returns is only about 0.13 (§IV "Pairs Trading RiskReturn Profile").

## Test setup

Sample: US stocks in daily CRSP files, share codes 10 and 11, July 1962–June 2009 (return sample July 1963–June 2009) (§II "Stock sample"). Screens: bottom NYSE-breakpoint size decile removed; closing prices below $5 in the formation year removed — though the Introduction states the price screen as "$1" (the discrepancy is in the paper, not resolved); stocks with one or more non-trading days or invalid prices/returns in the formation period removed; over the trading period, pairs with invalid or missing prices/returns are assigned zero returns. Membership is reconstructed from CRSP with these screens; the paper describes no other point-in-time reconstruction, and delisting-return handling and survivorship bias are not addressed. Sub-period convention: 1989 marks the emergence of the hedge fund industry, following GGR (2006) and Do and Faff (2010). Data sources: CRSP; Fama-French factors and momentum; Pastor-Stambaugh liquidity innovations (WRDS); Jones (2002) and ITG (2008, 2010) commissions; D'Avolio (2002) for the loan fee. Costs: commissions average 34 bps one-way 1963–2009 (Jones 2002 discounted 20%, ITG 2005–2009, interpolated 2001–2004; Table 2); market impact estimated ex-post from price moves following divergence at ~26 bps average (30 bps 1963–1988, 20 bps 1989–2009), implying one-way costs of 60 bps full sample, 81 bps 1963–1988, 33 bps 1989–2009, 28 bps 2007–2009 (§III). All Tables 4–9 numbers are monthly excess returns; no financing-cost adjustment beyond the loan fee is described; headline results are reported gross (Table 4) and net (Tables 5–9), both given. No strategy uses leverage explicitly; construction is equally weighted across pairs.

The authors conclude that the baseline GGR strategy loses its profitability net of costs; profitability survives only in refined, industry-matched, SSD-plus-NZC portfolios at much diminished levels; pairs trading is a lower-risk, lower-return alternative to intra-industry reversal; and both are largely dead after 2002, with the 2000–2002 bear market generating the bulk of recent profits (§IV Discussion, Conclusion). They favour the SSD∩NZC, Fama-French-industry-matched portfolios (24, 25, 27, 29) on both raw and risk-adjusted net grounds (Tables 5, 7).

## Robustness

The authors test: top-3-size-decile universe (Table 9 Panel A); the same with one-way costs reduced by 10 bps (Panel B); four sub-periods from 1989 (Table 8); four factor specifications (Tables 6–7); and exclusion of the smallest, cheapest, and illiquid names throughout. Restricting to large caps reduces top-4 alphas from 32 to 24 bps/month and renders the intra-industry strategy insignificant (alpha 9 bps); lower costs raise top-4 alphas to 37 bps. Profit decline post-2002 is attributed to smaller mispricings: average divergence per trade fell from 10.00% (2000–2002) to 8.24% (2007–2009), with 67% versus 55% of top-4 pairs converging at least once, and weighted profit per pair 1.23 percentage points lower per month (§IV). Factor loadings flip sign across the two bear markets (e.g., short-term reversal loading −0.0118 to +0.0299 for the top 4 under Model 1). The paper has no robustness section as such; these tests are distributed through §III–§IV.

## Specification search

The paper reports 29 pairs portfolios (Table 1) plus 1 intra-industry strategy, 4 factor models, 2 universes, and 4+ sub-periods, and reports results for all 29 portfolios in every main table (Tables 4–9), not a selected subset. Certain statistics are explicitly untabulated: turnover of 2.7 months, trades per pair of 2.9 (average) and 2.2 (Banks), risk-adjusted alphas of the intra-industry strategy (66, 51, 40 bps under Models 1, 3, 4), and the top-3-decile market-impact reduction of 3–4 bps (footnotes 12–13; §IV "Sensitivity Analysis"). The authors acknowledge data-snooping risk and state they deliberately retained GGR's implementation parameters (footnote 5).

## Limitations and future work

The authors' own limitations: their cost estimates are proxies assembled from the literature and "impossible to determine exactly" (footnote 7); they do not account for limits to arbitrage — fundamental (Shleifer-Vishny), synchronization (Abreu-Brunnermeier), crowd-trade (Stein 2009), and forced-unwind/leverage risk (Khandani-Lo, August 2007) — so pre-2003 profits may be compensation for arbitrageurs bearing these risks, and near-zero post-2002 alphas could reflect steeper arbitrageur losses (§IV Discussion). They suggest future evaluation of arbitrage strategies use asset-based style factors (Fung and Hsieh 2002, 2004). They also note profitability may have been competed away by the hedge fund industry's growth since the early 1990s.

## Separable variants for our pipeline

- GGR (2006) baseline: top-20 SSD pairs, 12-month formation / 6-month trading, 2-sigma divergence trigger, equal-weighted, overlapping monthly starts (Table 1, portfolio 1; §II).
- SSD-only refinements: top-50 pairs; pairs 11–30; pairs 11–60 — unconditional, sector, and 48-industry versions (Table 1, portfolios 2–4, 10–13, 19–22).
- SSD ∩ NZC intersections: 1st/2nd/3rd SSD vigintile ∩ 20th NZC vigintile, and 1st/2nd SSD decile ∩ 10th NZC decile — unconditional, sector, and 48-industry versions (Table 1, portfolios 5–9, 14–18, 23–27).
- Single-industry Utilities and Banks portfolios: top-20 NZC among top-50 SSD (Table 1, portfolios 28–29).
- Intra-industry reversal: Industry Losers ∩ Market Neutral minus Industry Winners ∩ Market Neutral, prior-month double sort, 48 industries (Table 4 note).

This list is the strategies again because the paper is a comparison; no variant beyond those named is present. NZC and SSD are the two matching metrics the paper sorts on, including all alternative parameterizations tested (vigintile/decile positions).
