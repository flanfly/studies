**Category: anomaly** (clock-phase-conditioned return predictability in an empirical microstructure setting; no tradable strategy is constructed). The paper also contributes a diagnostic (trade-size roundness) and a method (Autocorrelation Map), covered inline below.

## Claim

Using millisecond-stamped aggregate trade data for six Binance USDT-margined perpetual futures (BTC, ETH, XRP, SOL, DOGE, ADA) over January 1, 2021 to October 31, 2024 (1,400 calendar days) (Table 1), the paper documents that trading activity bursts at one-minute, five-minute, quarter-hour, and top-of-hour clock marks, and that the quarter-hour boundaries carry phase-specific serial dependence and return predictability. Trade-size roundness declines hierarchically in these burst windows (algorithmic participation signature; Table 2). Quarter-hour opening (first 10-second) returns are predictable out of sample: the lag-plus-technical-indicator specification attains mean OOS R² 3.367% and AUC 0.6011 over 2021-07-01 to 2024-10-31 (Table 3). Quarter-hour opening order imbalance predicts cumulative returns over four to twelve hours — significant at the 95% level for four of six contracts at every horizon (SOL at 4h and 12h and ADA at 4h only at the 90% level; SOL's 8h cell the only insignificant one) (§6.1.1, Figure 5) — and a two-stage decomposition shows the dominant contributor rotating from lagged flow (58% share at 4h) to the component spanned by observable price-volume signals (65% at 12h) (Table 5). Returns are reported gross; the authors state the predictable component averages about 0.5 bp per boundary, "about one tenth of a single standard-tier taker fee and one twentieth of a round trip" (Binance standard-tier fees: 5.0 bp taker, 2.0 bp maker) (§5.3, Table A.3, fn. 6).

## Fit with our constraints

Instrument class: cryptocurrency perpetual futures — directly in our space; no equity/options components. The return signals are directional (sign of the forecast, sign of order imbalance), so both sides are expressible as perp longs/shorts; no short-stock or short-options leg is required. Long and short legs are not reported separately — the paper never constructs a portfolio, so there is no leg decomposition. Leverage is not discussed anywhere. Replication needs: public Binance (and Bybit, for BTC/ETH robustness) aggregate-trade files — millisecond timestamp, price, quantity, `isBuyerMaker` — plus 15-minute OHLCV for the technical indicators; no proprietary data. The conditioning variable is computed from real-time trade data with no point-in-time reconstruction issue, and the baseline predictors are fixed at least 15 minutes before each boundary (§5.1). Contracts were selected "for their long and liquid trading histories" (§2); survivorship/delisting is not applicable (perpetuals, no expiration) and not discussed.

## Anomaly evidence

**The conditioning variable.** Clock phase: the quarter-hour boundaries, minutes *m* ∈ {0, 15, 30, 45} of the hour, and within them the first 10-second subperiod *b* = 1, defined as the "peak window" because activity "peaks within the first 10 seconds" of quarter-hour minutes (Figure 2, §3.1). The signal variables are built from trade prints: log return *r_t* = log(*P_t*) − log(*P*_{t−1}) with *P_t* the last traded price in interval *t*; signed order flow OF_t = Σ_{k∈t} V_k D_k with D_k = +1 if buyer-initiated (`isBuyerMaker` = False), −1 otherwise; and order imbalance OI_t = OF_t / Σ_{k∈t} V_k ∈ [−1, 1] (§2.1). The opening return response anchors on the last transaction price at or before the boundary and measures to the volume-weighted average transaction price over (T, T + 10s] (§5.1). Because the data are the public trades themselves, the gap between data becoming public and conditioning is zero; the forecast predictors exclude the final 15 minutes before the boundary (§5.1).

**How the anomaly is demonstrated.** Not a portfolio sort: the demonstration is boundary-versus-non-boundary conditioning within predictive regressions and event-time averages. (i) Burst magnitudes: averaged across the six contracts, the first 10 seconds of quarter-hour minutes have roughly 26% more trades, 32% higher dollar volume, and 26% larger absolute returns than the same interval of ordinary minutes, all significant at the 1% level (Appendix Table A.2). (ii) The Autocorrelation Map (ACM), a sign-based phase-resolved autocorrelation τ(k, m) ≡ E[sign(x_{d,h,m} x_{d,h,m+k})] for x ∈ {returns, order flow}, indexed by lag *k* and minute-of-hour *m*, shows a 15-minute "lattice" of positive autocorrelation at (*k*, *m*) with *k* ∈ {15, 30, ...} and *m* ∈ {0, 15, 30, 45}, plus negative lag-1 return autocorrelation at *m* ∈ {14, 29, 44, 59} (§4.1–4.2, Figure 3); the lattice is concentrated in *b* = 1 and nearly gone by *b* = 2 (§4.3, Figure 4, Figure A.8). (iii) Out-of-sample forecasting of opening returns (Table 3, §5.3). (iv) Horizon-specific OLS regressions of forward cumulative returns on OI interacted with nested clock-time dummies (eq. 2, §6.1). There is no value- or equal-weighting choice; regressions are run per asset and averaged across assets.

**Leg decomposition.** None exists: no long/short spread portfolio is formed. The spread-like object is the boundary-versus-baseline slope; sign-split estimates show predictive content in both positive and negative quarter-hour imbalance at medium horizons (Appendix Figure A.9). Nothing here requires a short leg, but equally nothing in the paper's presentation gives a short-only or long-only breakdown.

**What fails to explain it.** No factor model (CAPM, FF, Carhart, q-factor, Stambaugh–Yuan) is estimated — the predictability is time-series microstructure, not cross-sectional alpha. The robustness that stands in for factor controls: placebo phase shifts (shifted 15-minute grids anchored at minutes 2/17/32/47, 5/20/35/50, 7/22/37/52) concentrate far less dependence than the true grid (Figure A.7); the medium-horizon OI effect survives excluding the three daily funding-settlement openings (00:00, 08:00, 16:00 UTC; Table A.7); it survives separating the top-of-hour from minutes 15/30/45 (Figure A.10); bid-ask bounce is controlled by reversal dummies W_t = (η⁺_t, η⁻_t) from last-trade aggressor switches (§6.1); and results replicate on Bybit for BTC and ETH (Figures A.4–A.6).

**Where the return concentrates.** In the first 10 seconds after the quarter-hour boundary, and, for medium-horizon OI predictability, at the quarter-hour frequency specifically — one-minute and five-minute openings carry "much weaker effects" (§6.1.1; cf. abstract). All six contracts show the pattern; the paper reports no within-asset breakdown by trade size, price level, or liquidity, and no microcap/penny-stock issue arises — the smallest contract (ADA) still averages $544M daily dollar volume (Table 1).

**Persistence and decay.** The ACM shows dependence persisting across successive quarter-hour cycles (lags 15, 30, ... minutes, §4.4); within the minute it decays from *b* = 1 to *b* = 2 (§4.3). The OI predictive profile is nonmonotonic: negative over the first half hour (short-run reversal), then positive, peaking between eight and twelve hours (§6.1.1). The out-of-sample window spans 2021-07-01 to 2024-10-31; no subperiod decay analysis and no seasonality (January, turn-of-month) analysis is reported — the paper does not examine them.

**Frictions the authors acknowledge.** All figures are gross; fees are cited (5.0 bp taker, 2.0 bp maker standard tier) but no net-of-cost strategy is computed, on the stated grounds that the opening reference price "is not necessarily attainable" and a strategy would require assumptions about market impact, queue priority, and execution rules (§5.3). Turnover is not reported; a naive every-quarter-hour implementation would trade 96 times per day, but the authors do not propose one and describe the forecast as "an input to execution and liquidity provision rather than as a standalone trading strategy" (§5.3). No capacity analysis is given.

**Sample end date versus publication date.** Sample ends October 31, 2024; the document is dated July 20, 2026 (arXiv v2). About 21 months elapsed between sample end and this version.

## Test setup

Sample period January 1, 2021 – October 31, 2024 (1,400 days), six contracts, unit of observation a 10-second bar (§2, Table 1). Out-of-sample evaluation runs 2021-07-01 to 2024-10-31 with roughly 117,000 quarter-hour boundary observations per asset: rolling walk-forward LASSO (continuous return) or ℓ1-penalized logistic (direction), six-month training windows, monthly refits (40 per asset), penalty λ from a grid 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹ chosen on a chronological 20% tail of each training window (§5.2.3). Data: Binance public aggregate-trade files; Binance bid-ask data (May 16, 2023 – March 30, 2024) used only for the anchoring robustness (Table A.14); Bybit data for BTC/ETH in ACM robustness. Rare missing-data episodes in the official Binance files are noted as negligible and corrected by Binance by March 16, 2026 (§2). OOS R² is measured against the zero forecast (§A.4); DM statistics use HAC lag 6 (§5.4); predictive-regression standard errors are Newey–West with Bartlett kernel and bandwidth equal to the horizon (§6.1, Table A.4). Returns are gross; no financing, funding-rate, or borrowing cost is netted anywhere.

The authors conclude the quarter-hour pattern is "not a top-of-hour effect" (Appendix A.11), that "the quarter-hour predictability" of order flow "is not exhausted by same-phase lagged returns" (§5.3), and that the horizon rotation identifies persistent boundary flow as the short-horizon source and observable price-volume state as the long-horizon source of predictive content (§6.2). Metrics commonly reported in strategy papers are absent: no Sharpe ratio anywhere, no net-of-cost return, no turnover, no t-statistic on OOS R² (DM tests substitute).

## Robustness

For the ACM: cross-asset maps (Figures A.1–A.3), Bybit replication for BTC/ETH (Figures A.4–A.6), placebo phase shifts (Figure A.7), within-minute block profile (Figure A.8), and a comparison of conventional versus phase-specific autocorrelations showing the quarter-hour structure is "almost entirely obscured in the aggregate autocorrelation" (Figure A.11). For the forecast: mid-price versus trade-price anchoring on the restricted bid-ask window — combined specification strongest for every asset under both anchors (Table A.14). For the OI regressions: 15-minute-only specification (Table A.4), sign of OI (Table A.5), Hodrick (1992) standard errors (Table A.6), exclusion of funding-settlement openings (Table A.7), and top-of-hour separation (Figure A.10) — "across all four checks the medium-horizon effect is positive in every contract-horizon cell," with SOL's 8h cell insignificant throughout and a few cells weakening in the sign specification (§A.7). One robustness check is described but unreported: updating forecast predictors through T − 1 minute "generally improves out-of-sample performance" (§5.1, §5.3). No subperiod robustness is reported.

## Specification search

The paper reports three predictor sets (12 lags, 28 indicators, 40-feature union) for six assets at monthly refit, a LASSO λ grid of unspecified granularity over 10⁻⁵–10⁻¹, eleven forecast horizons for the OI regressions, and seven robustness variants of the CFE specification; results for all six assets are shown in most tables, though per-cell bootstrap intervals are deliberately omitted in Table A.11 and point shares are described as "imprecisely estimated" (§6.2). The technical-indicator menu is fully specified (5 momentum, 9 trend, 10 volume, 4 volatility; §A.3). Some variants are reported only qualitatively (the T−1min predictor update).

## Limitations and future work

The authors' own: aggregate trade data lack trader identifiers, order-level messages, and account labels, so algorithmic attribution rests on "timing and behavioral diagnostics rather than direct attribution" (§7); same-trader persistence cannot be separated from cross-trader correlation (§5); order-book data are absent, so the liquidity-supply channel is not examined (§4.4); the two-stage decomposition is "empirical rather than structural" and public-signal content may be absorbed into lagged flow, so the estimated public contribution is a conservative measure (§6.2). Suggested future work: order-level data for sharper tests, and comparing the same asset across crypto-native and traditional venues to separate venue-specific from asset-specific effects (§7).

## Separable variants for our pipeline

- Quarter-hour opening-return forecast, Lag block: 12 same-phase lags R(T − 15k min, Δ), k = 1..12 (§5.1).
- TI28 technical-indicator block, fully enumerated (§A.3), as a standalone 28-feature market-state signal.
- Lag+TI28 combined forecast (§5.3).
- Horizon-specific OI predictive regression with nested clock-time dummies, eq. (2), CFE₁₅ₘᵢₙ at 4–12h (§6.1).
- Lagged-flow component: OI projected on its own 12 quarter-hour lags (§6.2, eq. A.1).
- Public-signal component: OI projected on TI28 (§6.2, eq. A.1).
- TZshare(z) trade-size roundness as a real-time participation/algorithmic-activity measure, not a return signal (§3.2, eq. 1).
- The ACM as a diagnostic for phase-resolved serial dependence (§4.1).
