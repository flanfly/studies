Category: comparison — three trend-following system designs (European, American, TSMOM) are constructed, evaluated against each other and against the SG Trend Index, and, in the paper's core contribution, reduced to a single analytical framework whose closed forms are derived and verified by simulation and on futures data.

### Claim

The paper presents a unified framework for trend-following (TF) systems, classifying them as European (continuous EWMA signal), American (breakout with stops), and Time Series Momentum (Definition 3.1, §3). For the European system it derives an exact sample-path identity for the cumulative return (Proposition 4.3) and closed-form expected return, gross Sharpe ratio, leading-order net Sharpe ratio, turnover proxy, and — under white noise — skewness of the aggregated return. The expected return decomposes into an autocorrelation term and a drift term (Corollary 4.9, 4.10): a TF system is profitable at zero drift whenever the ν-weighted autocorrelation sum exceeds one, so "trend-following alpha is excess spectral mass at low frequencies," read through a Poisson kernel whose bandwidth is set by the filter span (§5.1, eq. 5.4). Long memory (ARFIMA, d = 0.02) alone makes the system profitable, even under short-term mean reversion, once the span exceeds one month (§6.3). On market data, the sample autocorrelation function and drift of volatility-normalized returns reproduce the realized in-sample Sharpe ratios of the European system on 84 liquid futures contracts with a pooled correlation of 0.99 and a regression slope of 0.96 (§7.3, Figure 7.3). The three systems generate similar performance — annualized Sharpe ratios of 0.47, 0.50, and 0.55 versus 0.47 for the SG Trend Index, with Ledoit–Wolf p-values of 0.96, 0.82, and 0.62 (§7.2) — and correlate with the index at 80% on average, with the European and American systems correlating at 95% (§3.2, §7.2). Sample period: backtests run 1 January 1997 to 10 July 2026; the index comparison runs 31 December 1999 to 10 July 2026 (§7.1, §7.2).

### Fit with our constraints

Instruments are futures only: 84 liquid contracts across equities (21), bonds (16), STIR (4), FX (10), energy (7), metals (6), agriculture (20) (Table 7.1). All three strategies are long-short by construction but the short leg is a short futures position implied by the sign of a continuous or binary signal; it needs no short stock or short options. Long and short legs are not reported separately — positions are a single signed exposure per contract. Leverage: volatility targeting with σtarget = 15% and weight w_t^{vt} = σtarget/(√a σ_t), a = 260; no explicit leverage cap is described (§4, eq. 4.2). Replication needs daily futures data (high/low/close for ATR) converted to USD via the FX convention of eq. (2.3), continuous front/second-contract series stitched at rolls, and the volume-based cost schedule of Table 7.2; the dataset is in the companion repository. Survivorship and delisting are not addressed. Headline comparison numbers are net of transaction costs and 2%/20% fees; the attribution test of §7.3 uses gross returns.

### Strategies

**European TF system** (Definition 4.1, §3.1, §4). Signal: the variance-preserving EWMA filter of volatility-normalized returns, S_t = L̂^{(ν)}(z_t) or the long-short filter L̂S^{(ν1,ν2)}(z_t) (eq. 4.1), where z_t = r_t/σ_{t−1} (eq. 2.14), σ_t is EWMA volatility of r_t² with span 33 days (eq. 2.13), ν = 1 − 2/(span+1) (eq. 2.5), and L̂^{(ν)} = √((1+ν)/(1−ν)) L^{(ν)} (eq. 2.7); the long-short filter is defined in eq. (2.9) with loadings (2.10). Position weight: w_t = S_t σtarget/(√a σ_t) (eq. 4.2); daily return f_t = (σtarget/√a) S_{t−1} z_t (eq. 4.3) — a one-day lag between signal date and trade date, with end-of-day rebalancing at the close ("orders are sampled and submitted shortly before the session close," footnote 2). The authors' rationale is serial dependence in volatility-normalized returns under the physical measure, consistent with no-arbitrage via the Dalang–Morton–Willinger theorem (§1); they frame it neither as risk-based nor behavioural, and do not identify the counterparty. The expected annual return is F̄_{1y} = h_{1y}[Σ_{m=0}^∞ ν^m ρ(m) − 1] + (lσtarget/√a)(μ^z_an)² (eq. 4.22). Chosen parameters: long-short LS(250,20), realized costs 1.7% per year (§7.1); the signal-turnover proxy (Proposition 4.6) is 393% per year for the single 250-day filter and 88% for LS(250,20) (§4.4). Universe screens: skip the first 250 days and require at least six years of history (§7.3).

**American TF system** (Definition A.3, Appendix A.1). EWMA filters on prices, s̄^{(ν1)} (slow) and s̄^{(ν2)} (fast); a long is initiated when s̄^{(ν2)} > s̄^{(ν1)} + ω·ATR_t (eq. A.6), a short when s̄^{(ν2)} < s̄^{(ν1)} − ω·ATR_t (eq. A.8); position size p^{long} = R·s_t/ATR_t with risk multiple R and stop-loss width p (eqs. A.7, A.9); exits require both stop breach and signal-off (eqs. A.10, A.12), with trailing stops updated as s^{long}_t = max{s^{long}_{t−1}, s_t − p·ATR_t} (eq. A.11). Positions are binary and fixed at inception. ATR uses N = 33 days (eq. A.2); ω = p = 5 and spans 250/20 are the chosen settings (§7.1). Volatility-normalized turnover is 125–200%, about 50% lower than the European and TSMOM systems, because the system trades discretely (§7.2, panel B2 of Figure 7.2).

**TSMOM system** (Definition A.4, Appendix A.2). Generalizes Moskowitz et al. (2012) to w_{t′} = (σtarget/σ^an_{t′−1}) S_{t′} with S_{t′} = (1/√(ML)) Σ_{m=1}^M b_{t′−(m−1)L}, b_{t″} = Σ_{t=t″−L+1}^{t″} sign(r_t/σ^an_{t−1}) (eq. A.16), rebalanced on the grid of multiples of L days and forward-filled to the daily grid, with no same-day look-ahead. The signal value depends on (L, M) only through the product ML; L sets the rebalancing frequency. Chosen parameters L = M = 10 (§7.1); the paper recommends L ∈ (5, 30) with weekly-to-monthly rebalancing (§3).

**What the comparison establishes.** All three designs monetize the same underlying trend signal: the European closed form ranks the realized performance of the American and TSMOM implementations at design-specific discounts (TSMOM: pooled correlation 0.89, slope 0.73, near the Gaussian sign-moment benchmark √(2/π) ≈ 0.80; American: correlation 0.92, slope 0.61 at spans above one month, §7.3), and none of the three delivers a Sharpe ratio statistically different from the SG Trend Index (§7.2). Median realized European Sharpe ratios run 0.48 (5-day span) to 0.39 (two-year span), 4–14% below the total prediction, with the autocorrelation channel contributing medians from 0.55 to 0.33 and the drift channel adding up to 0.08, growing with span (§7.3, Figure 7.4).

### Test setup

Sample period: 1 January 1997 to 10 July 2026 (grid backtests, §7.1); 31 December 1999 to 10 July 2026 (SG Trend comparison, §7.2). Instruments: 84 continuous futures contracts (Table 7.1), base currency USD, non-USD returns converted by the FX ratio of eq. (2.3). Volatility: EWMA of daily returns, span 33 days, for European and TSMOM; ATR period 33 days for American. Costs: one-way volume-based costs by asset class and period following Exhibit B1 of Hurst et al. (2017), e.g. equities 34bp/11bp/6bp, commodities 58bp/19bp/10bp across the three cost periods (Table 7.2); 2%/20% management/performance fees applied to simulated systems for the index comparison (§7.2). The Sharpe ratio uses the arithmetic daily-moment convention of eq. (5.1) on excess returns with no risk-free adjustment; the Bear Sharpe conditions on the 16% worst quarters of a 60/40 equity/bond benchmark (§7.1). Gross and net figures are both reported (net in §7.2, gross in §7.3). Survivorship and delisting are not addressed; the SG Trend Index comparison "modestly favors the index" because the index includes interest income that cannot be stripped out (§7.2). Monte Carlo verification: 1000 paths of 50 years of daily returns, volatility span 33, σtarget = 15%, cost c = 20bp per unit of volatility-normalized turnover (§6).

The authors' conclusion: TF performance on liquid futures is primarily an autocorrelation phenomenon with a drift share that grows with span, and the closed forms enable span selection from the autocorrelation structure, kurtosis, and cost level (§8).

### Robustness

Monte Carlo verification across white noise, AR-1, and ARFIMA processes with Gaussian and Student-t(6) innovations (excess kurtosis 3, near the empirical median of 3.8): analytic values stay within 0.05 of simulation; heavy tails lower the pooled gross Sharpe ratio by at most 0.009 and 20bp costs by at most 0.02 (Table 6.1, §6.4). The skewness closed form holds without Gaussianity (third-moment pairing uses only second moments; a nonzero innovation third moment adds a term decaying as T^{−1/2}, §7.4) and costs of 100bp change white-noise skewness by less than 0.002 (§7.4). Grid backtests cover 64 configurations per system and are presented as a sensitivity analysis, not selection, because the grid maximum overstates out-of-sample value (§7.1). The EWMA lag-1 autocorrelation of z_t across the universe declines from about 0.04 in the 1990s to about 0.01 after 2010 (§7.3); no subperiod robustness of the backtests beyond this is reported. No out-of-sample test is conducted — "the out-of-sample application is a subject for future research" (§7.3).

### Specification search

Reported: a 64-configuration grid per system (spans grid for European and American, (L, M) grid for TSMOM, Figure 7.1, §7.1); span labels 1w=5, 2w=10, 1m=21, 3m=63, 6m=125, 1y=250, 2y=500 days (eq. 6.1); attribution over spans 5–520 days (§7.3); Monte Carlo verification over 1000 paths of 50 years (Table 6.1) and skewness simulations over 400,000 and 100,000 paths (Figure 7.5); an in-sample calibrated parameter search mapping American and TSMOM lookbacks onto the European filter (§7.3). Results are reported only for selected specifications (the chosen spans and the figure grids), not for every cell.

### Limitations and future work

Authors' own: the in-sample attribution requires a debiased or shrunk drift for forward-looking use ("a forward-looking application should debias or shrink it," §7.3); the net Sharpe results are leading-order approximations under a signal-turnover proxy, since the full pipeline turnover exceeds the proxy by a factor of 1.6–2.3 for the long-short filter at the 33-day volatility span (§4.4); the American and TSMOM mappings are in-sample calibrated rather than independent validation (§7.3); the general skewness derivation for AR-1/ARFIMA and the asymptotics of the horizon profile are deferred to a follow-up paper (§7.4, Appendix D); the diversification benefits of TF portfolios for long-only investors are in a companion paper (§8); extension to nonlinear (sign-based) filters is noted (§8).

### Separable variants for our pipeline

This is a comparison paper, so the variants are largely the strategies themselves: (1) European single-filter system at span ν (Definition 4.1, §4); (2) European long-short system LS(ν1, ν2) (eqs. 2.9, 4.1); (3) TSMOM system at period length L and count M (Definition A.4); (4) American system at (long, short, ω, p) (Definition A.3); (5) the attribution formula of eq. (5.12) itself, which is a standalone tool mapping a sample autocorrelation function and drift onto a predicted Sharpe ratio (§7.3).
